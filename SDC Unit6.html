Intuition 1 · Why Current Mirrors Replace Resistors in Integrated Circuits

In discrete circuits, resistors set bias currents and collector loads. On a silicon integrated circuit chip, resistors are expensive, bulky, and inaccurate. A 100 kΩ diffused resistor consumes over 10,000 µm² of precious silicon area, possesses a poor absolute tolerance of ±20% to ±30%, and drifts severely with temperature. In stark contrast, an NPN transistor consumes less than 100 µm² (100 times smaller!) and adjacent transistors on the same silicon die match their base-emitter voltages V_{BE} within 1 mV. Current mirrors exploit this near-perfect matching to replace passive resistors with compact, stable active current sources and active loads.

Intuition 2 · The Widlar Breakthrough for Microamperes

To bias the sensitive input stages of an operational amplifier with 10 µA to 20 µA using a simple current mirror from a 10 V supply, one would need a reference resistor R_{ref} = (10 - 0.7 V) / 20 µA = 465 kΩ! Fabricating nearly half a megohm on a chip is economically prohibitive. Robert Widlar's stroke of genius was adding a small resistor R_{E} to only the output transistor. Dropping just ~100 mV across R_{E} reduces V_{BE2} relative to V_{BE1}. Because collector current depends exponentially on V_{BE}, that tiny 100 mV drop reduces current by 50-fold (from 1 mA down to 20 µA) using an R_{E} of only 5.09 kΩ, slashing required resistor area by 97%!

Intuition 3 · The Wilson Negative-Feedback Loop

A simple BJT mirror has an output resistance limited by the Early effect to r_{o} ≈ 100 kΩ and a base-current error of order 2/β (≈ 2%). George Wilson added a third transistor and closed a local negative feedback loop inside the mirror: any rise in output current is sensed by the bottom transistors and immediately robs base current from the output transistor. This internal negative feedback squares the base current error to order 2/β² (≈ 0.02%) and boosts the dynamic output resistance by β/2 into megohms (R_{o} ≈ 5 MΩ).

Intuition 4 · The Differential Pair as the Universal Op-Amp Front End

A conventional common-emitter stage is fundamentally single-ended: it cannot distinguish between an input sensor signal and ground noise, supply ripple, or thermal V_{BE} drift (-2 mV/°C). The differential pair amplifies only the difference between its two input terminals (v_{id} = v_{1} - v_{2}) while completely suppressing any voltage common to both (v_{cm}). Because of thermal and device symmetry, temperature drift affects both sides identically and cancels out at the differential output. Furthermore, direct DC coupling eliminates coupling capacitors, allowing amplification down to 0 Hz.

Intuition 5 · The Split-Supply Magic (±V_{CC})

By powering the differential amplifier from symmetrical dual supplies (+V_{CC} and -V_{EE}), both input bases can be held directly at 0 V DC ground potential while the joined emitters rest at -0.7 V. This completely eliminates input coupling capacitors and bulky voltage-divider bias networks, allowing external transducers, bio-electrodes, and previous DC stages to connect directly to the inputs with zero DC offset voltage.

The Basic BJT Current Mirror

Winter 2025 SDC-I Q6 (10 Marks)Diode-Connected BJTFinite Beta ErrorCompliance Voltage
Intuition · Why Diode Connection Works

Tying the collector of Q_{1} directly to its own base forces V_{CE1} = V_{BE1} ≈ 0.7 V. Since the collector-base junction voltage V_{CB1} = 0 V, Q_{1} is guaranteed to operate at the edge of the forward-active region (no saturation). It automatically adjusts its V_{BE1} to absorb whatever reference current I_{ref} flows through R_{ref}. Because Q_{2}'s base and emitter are tied in parallel with Q_{1}, Q_{2} experiences the exact same V_{BE} and replicates the identical collector current into the load.

    I_{ref} = I_{C} + [[I_{C}|β]] + [[I_{C}|β]] = I_{C} · (1 + [[2|β]])

    I_{O} = [[I_{ref}|1 + [[2|β]]]] = I_{ref} · [[β|β + 2]]

    I_{O} = I_{C2} · (1 + [[V_{CE2}|V_{A}]]) ⇒ r_{o} = [[V_{A} + V_{CE2}|I_{O}]] ≈ [[V_{A}|I_{O}]]
    The small-signal output resistance of the basic mirror is therefore R_{o} ≈ r_{o}.

    V_{O(min)} = V_{CE2(sat)} ≈ 0.2 V to 0.3 V

Quick check: Why does tying the collector to the base prevent Q_{1} from saturating?

In saturation, both the base-emitter and base-collector junctions must be forward-biased (V_{CB} < -0.4 V for NPN). When the collector is physically shorted to the base, V_{CB} = V_{C} - V_{B} = 0 V. A junction with zero applied voltage cannot be forward-biased. Thus Q_{1} operates strictly at the active/saturation boundary, conducting active collector current.

Topic 2

The Widlar Current Source

Summer 2026 Q6 (10 Marks)Sub-Milliampere BiasingLogarithmic Emitter DegenerationSilicon Area Economy
Intuition · The Logarithmic Leverage of R_{E}

In analog IC design, bias currents of 10 µA to 20 µA are required to achieve low power dissipation and high input impedance. In a basic current mirror, generating 20 µA from a 10 V supply requires a huge 465 kΩ resistor that would consume colossal silicon die area. Robert Widlar inserted an emitter resistor R_{E} into only the output transistor Q_{2}. By dropping just ~100 mV across R_{E}, V_{BE2} is made slightly smaller than V_{BE1}. Because bipolar collector current depends exponentially on V_{BE}, that tiny 100 mV drop reduces the output current by a factor of 50 using a compact 5.09 kΩ resistor!

1. Systematic Mathematical Derivation of Widlar Design Equation

  1. KVL around the Base-Emitter Loop: Tracing the voltage loop from ground, through Q_{1}'s base-emitter junction, through Q_{2}'s base-emitter junction, and through R_{E} back to ground:
    V_{B1} = V_{BE1} and V_{B2} = V_{BE2} + I_{E2}R_{E}
    Since the two bases are tied together (V_{B1} = V_{B2}):
    V_{BE1} = V_{BE2} + I_{E2}R_{E} ⇒ V_{BE1} - V_{BE2} = I_{E2}R_{E}
    Assuming high current gain β >> 1, the emitter current I_{E2} ≈ I_{C2} = I_{O}:
    V_{BE1} - V_{BE2} ≈ I_{O}R_{E}
  2. BJT Exponential Current Relationship: For matched transistors at the same die temperature:
    I_{C1} = I_{S} · e^{V_{BE1}/V_{T}} ⇒ V_{BE1} = V_{T} · ln([[I_{C1}|I_{S}]])
    I_{C2} = I_{S} · e^{V_{BE2}/V_{T}} ⇒ V_{BE2} = V_{T} · ln([[I_{C2}|I_{S}]])
  3. Subtracting the Base-Emitter Voltages:
    V_{BE1} - V_{BE2} = V_{T} · ln([[I_{C1}|I_{S}]]) - V_{T} · ln([[I_{C2}|I_{S}]]) = V_{T} · ln([[I_{C1}|I_{C2}]])
    Since I_{C1} ≈ I_{ref} and I_{C2} = I_{O}:
    V_{BE1} - V_{BE2} = V_{T} · ln([[I_{ref}|I_{O}]])
  4. Equating KVL to the Logarithmic Voltage:
    I_{O}R_{E} = V_{T} · ln([[I_{ref}|I_{O}]])
  5. The Widlar Design Equation for R_{E}: Rearranging gives the explicit design formula:
    R_{E} = [[V_{T}|I_{O}]] · ln([[I_{ref}|I_{O}]])

2. Critical Engineering Observations

  • Direct Design vs. Transcendental Analysis: If reference current I_{ref} and desired output current I_{O} are known, R_{E} is calculated in closed form. However, if R_{E} and I_{ref} are given, finding I_{O} requires solving a transcendental equation iteratively.
  • Higher Output Resistance: Resistor R_{E} introduces negative current-series feedback into Q_{2}, multiplying the dynamic output resistance:
    R_{o} ≈ r_{o2} · [1 + g_{m2}(R_{E} || r_{π2})] ≈ r_{o2} · (1 + g_{m2}R_{E})
    Since g_{m2}R_{E} = (I_{O}/V_{T}) · [(V_{T}/I_{O}) · ln(I_{ref}/I_{O})] = ln(I_{ref}/I_{O}) ≈ 3 to 5, the output resistance is 4 to 6 times higher than a simple mirror!
  • Compliance Voltage Requirement: Because the emitter of Q_{2} rests at V_{RE} = I_{O}R_{E} above ground, the output compliance requires slightly more headroom:
    V_{O(min)} ≥ V_{CE2(sat)} + I_{O}R_{E} ≈ 0.2 V + 0.1 V = 0.3 V

Quick check: Why must the natural logarithm ln be used instead of log10?

The BJT collector current follows Boltzmann thermodynamics: I_{C} = I_{S} · e^{V_{BE}/V_{T}}, where the base of the exponent is the natural mathematical constant e (2.71828). The Wilson Current Mirror

Winter 2025 Q6 (10 Marks)Internal Negative FeedbackOrder β² AccuracyMegohm Output Resistance
Intuition · Stabilizing Current via Negative Feedback

The simple two-transistor current mirror suffers from two serious defects: (1) base currents cause a ~2% error, and (2) Early effect limits output resistance to ~100 kΩ. George Wilson added a third transistor (Q_{3}) that closes a negative feedback loop: If output current I_{O} attempts to rise due to output voltage fluctuations, that extra current flows through Q_{3}'s emitter directly into Q_{2}. This increases V_{BE2} and V_{BE1}, causing Q_{1} to conduct more current away from Q_{3}'s base, which immediately throttles Q_{3} back down! This feedback squares the base-current cancellation error to order 2/β² and multiplies output resistance by β/2 into the megohm realm.

1. Systematic Current Ratio Derivation

  1. Matched Transistor Base Currents: Transistors Q_{1} and Q_{2} share identical base-emitter connections (V_{BE1} = V_{BE2}). Assuming matched devices and identical β:
    I_{C1} = I_{C2} = I_{C} and I_{B1} = I_{B2} = [[I_{C}|β]]
  2. KCL at the Emitter of Output Transistor Q_{3}: The emitter current I_{E3} flows into the collector of Q_{2} and supplies the bases of both Q_{1} and Q_{2}:
    I_{E3} = I_{C2} + I_{B1} + I_{B2} = I_{C} + [[I_{C}|β]] + [[I_{C}|β]] = I_{C} · [[β + 2|β]]
  3. Relating Emitter Current to Output Collector Current: For transistor Q_{3}, where I_{C3} = I_{O}:
    I_{E3} = [[β + 1|β]] · I_{C3} = [[β + 1|β]] · I_{O}
  4. Equating Expressions for I_{E3}:
    [[β + 1|β]] · I_{O} = [[β + 2|β]] · I_{C} ⇒ I_{C} = [[β + 1|β + 2]] · I_{O}
  5. KCL at the Reference Input Node: The input current I_{in} splits into collector current I_{C1} and the base current of Q_{3}:
    I_{in} = I_{C1} + I_{B3} = I_{C} + [[I_{O}|β]]
    Substitute the expression for I_{C}:
    I_{in} = ([[β + 1|β + 2]] + [[1|β]]) · I_{O} = [[β(β + 1) + (β + 2)|β(β + 2)]] · I_{O} = [[β^{2} + 2β + 2|β(β + 2)]] · I_{O}
  6. Final Wilson Output Current Equation: Inverting the relation yields:
    I_{O} = [[β^{2} + 2β|β^{2} + 2β + 2]] · I_{in} = (1 - [[2|β^{2} + 2β + 2]]) · I_{in}
  7. Order 1/β² Fractional Error:
    [[I_{in} - I_{O}|I_{in}]] = [[2|β^{2} + 2β + 2]] ≈ [[2|β^{2}]] (for β >> 1)
    For β = 100, the error is 2/10,202 ≈ 0.0196%! Compared to 1.96% for the simple mirror, the Wilson mirror is 100 times more accurate.

2. Dynamic Output Resistance

The small-signal output resistance looking into the collector of Q_{3} is derived by applying a test voltage v_{x} and solving for i_{x}. Due to local series-shunt negative feedback:

R_{o} ≈ [[β · r_{o}|2]] = [[β · V_{A}|2 · I_{O}]]

For a basic mirror, R_{o} = r_{o} ≈ 100 kΩ. For a Wilson mirror with β = 100, R_{o} ≈ (100)(100 kΩ)/2 = 5.0 MΩ (a 50-fold enhancement!).

3. Compliance Voltage Trade-Off

The cascade of Q_{3} atop Q_{2} increases the minimum operating voltage required at the output terminal:

V_{O(min)} ≥ V_{BE3} + V_{CE2(sat)} ≈ 0.7 V + 0.2 V = 0.9 V

While the basic mirror can operate down to ~0.2 V, the Wilson mirror requires ~0.9 V of headroom.

Topic 4

Limitations of the CE Amplifier & Why the Differential Pair is Preferred

Winter 2025 Q6 OR (10 Marks)Single-Ended vs DifferentialDC DriftMiller EffectOp-Amp Front End
Intuition · The Vulnerability of Single-Ended Stages

The Common-Emitter (CE) amplifier is an effective general-purpose AC amplifier, but it is fundamentally crippled when used in direct-coupled precision systems or as an operational amplifier input stage. Because it is single-ended, it cannot distinguish between a true input signal and ambient 50 Hz power hum, supply voltage ripple, or temperature-induced transistor drift. The differential amplifier overcomes every single limitation of the CE stage by exploiting bilateral symmetry.

1. Detailed Analysis of CE Amplifier Limitations

  1. Fundamentally Single-Ended (Zero Common-Mode Rejection): A CE amplifier measures voltage between a single base node and signal ground. Any noise, supply ripple, or ground-bounce voltage appearing in the circuit is directly amplified along with the input signal. It has no mechanism to reject common interference.
  2. Coupling Capacitors Block DC and Low Frequencies: In multi-stage CE amplifiers, stages are separated by coupling and bypass capacitors to prevent DC bias interactions. However, capacitors establish a high-pass cutoff frequency:
    f_{L} = [[1|2π R_{eq}C]]
    Consequently, a capacitor-coupled CE amplifier cannot amplify true DC (0 Hz) or slowly changing biological/sensor voltages. Furthermore, physical capacitors larger than ~50 pF cannot be fabricated inside integrated circuits.
  3. Direct Coupling Causes Catastrophic DC Bias Drift: If coupling capacitors are omitted, the collector DC voltage of one stage sets the base DC voltage of the next. Thermal variations in base-emitter voltage (dV_{BE}/dT ≈ -2 mV/°C) and current gain β cause the quiescent point to drift with temperature. In high-gain multi-stage cascades, this DC drift is amplified until the output stage saturates at V_{CC} or cuts off at ground.
  4. Miller Effect Restricts High-Frequency Bandwidth: In an inverting CE stage with voltage gain A_{v}, the collector-base capacitance C_{μ} is multiplied at the input port by the Miller effect:
    C_{M,in} = C_{μ}(1 - A_{v}) = C_{μ}(1 + |A_{v}|)
    For a stage with |A_{v}| = 100 and C_{μ} = 2 pF, the effective input capacitance becomes 202 pF, drastically lowering the upper 3-dB cutoff frequency f_{H}.
  5. Parameter Sensitivity & Distortion: The voltage gain of a simple CE stage is A_{v} = -g_{m}R_{C} = -I_{C}R_{C}/V_{T}, which is directly proportional to quiescent current and inversely proportional to absolute temperature. It is also limited to linear signal swings of only ±10 mV before severe harmonic distortion occurs.

Quick check: Why does thermal drift cancel out in a differential pair?

Both transistors Q_{1} and Q_{2} are fabricated micrometers apart on the same silicon die and share identical thermal environments. If temperature rises, both base-emitter voltages decrease at the exact same rate (-2 mV/°C). Consequently, both collector currents shift by identical amounts ΔI_{C1} = ΔI_{C2}. At the balanced output, v_{od} = (V_{CC} - I_{C1}R_{C}) - (V_{CC} - I_{C2}R_{C}) = (I_{C2} - I_{C1})R_{C} = 0 V. The thermal drift is a pure common-mode signal and cancels completely!

Intuition · The Beauty of Dual Supplies

In single-supply systems, input bases must be biased at half the supply (e.g. +6 V on a 12 V rail) using resistor dividers, which requires blocking capacitors at the inputs. By using split supplies (+V_{CC} and -V_{EE}), both input bases sit comfortably at 0 V DC ground potential. The joined emitters rest at -0.7 V, and the tail resistor drops the rest of the voltage down to -V_{EE}. With zero input voltage, the circuit is perfectly balanced, and the differential output voltage is ideally 0.00 V.

1. Quiescent DC Analysis Steps

  1. Input Base DC Potentials: With both inputs connected to ground or signal sources with zero DC offset:
    V_{B1} = V_{B2} = 0 V
  2. Emitter Junction DC Voltage: Since both forward-biased base-emitter junctions are connected to the joined emitter node:
    V_{E} = V_{B} - V_{BE} = 0 V - 0.7 V = -0.7 V
  3. Voltage Across Tail Resistor R_{E}: The tail resistor connects from the joined emitter node (V_{E} = -0.7 V) to the negative supply rail (-V_{EE}):
    V_{RE} = V_{E} - (-V_{EE}) = V_{EE} - V_{BE} = V_{EE} - 0.7 V
    (Note: V_{EE} denotes the positive magnitude of the negative rail, e.g. 12 V for a -12 V supply).
  4. Total Quiescent Tail Current I_{T}:
    I_{T} = [[V_{EE} - V_{BE}|R_{E}]] ≈ [[V_{EE} - 0.7 V|R_{E}]]
  5. Current Splitting in Matched Transistors: By perfect circuit symmetry, the total tail current divides equally between the two branches:
    I_{E1} = I_{E2} = [[I_{T}|2]] ⇒ I_{C1} = I_{C2} = α · [[I_{T}|2]] ≈ [[I_{T}|2]] (for β >> 1)
  6. Quiescent Collector Voltages:
    V_{C1} = V_{CC} - I_{C1}R_{C} = V_{CC} - [[I_{T}|2]] · R_{C}
    V_{C2} = V_{CC} - I_{C2}R_{C} = V_{CC} - [[I_{T}|2]] · R_{C}
  7. Quiescent Differential DC Output: The balanced output voltage between the two collectors is:
    V_{OD} = V_{C1} - V_{C2} = 0 V
    This zero-volt DC quiescent differential output is the hallmark of the differential pair.

Differential and Common-Mode Signal Representations

Intuition · Decomposing Any Two Signals into Odd and Even Modes

When two voltages v_{1} and v_{2} enter a differential amplifier, they can always be decomposed mathematically into two orthogonal components: an anti-symmetric differential-mode signal v_{id} (the pure difference we want to amplify) and a symmetric common-mode signal v_{cm} (the average noise or interference we want to reject).

1. Mathematical Decomposition

  1. Differential Input Voltage (v_{id}): Defined as the algebraic difference across the two inputs:
    v_{id} = v_{1} - v_{2}
  2. Common-Mode Input Voltage (v_{cm}): Defined as the arithmetic average of the two inputs:
    v_{cm} = [[v_{1} + v_{2}|2]]
  3. Constituent Signal Synthesis: Reconstructing the original inputs from their modes:
    v_{1} = v_{cm} + [[v_{id}|2]] and v_{2} = v_{cm} - [[v_{id}|2]]
    This shows that input 1 receives the common-mode voltage plus half the differential voltage, while input 2 receives the common-mode voltage minus half the differential voltage.
  4. General Amplifier Output Equation: By superposition in the linear small-signal regime:
    v_{o} = A_{d}v_{id} + A_{c}v_{cm} = A_{d}(v_{1} - v_{2}) + A_{c} · [[v_{1} + v_{2}|2]]
    where A_{d} is the differential voltage gain and A_{c} is the common-mode voltage gain.
  5. Factoring Out the CMRR: Expressing output voltage in terms of Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR = |A_{d}/A_{c}|):
    v_{o} = A_{d}v_{id} · (1 + [[1|CMRR]] · [[v_{cm}|v_{id}]])
    This formula reveals that even when the common-mode noise v_{cm} is hundreds of times larger than the weak differential sensor signal v_{id}, a sufficiently high CMRR renders the error term negligible!

Intuition · The AC Virtual Ground

Under pure differential drive, input 1 is driven by +v_{id}/2 and input 2 is driven by -v_{id}/2. As Q_{1}'s emitter current increases by Δi_{e}, Q_{2}'s emitter current decreases by the exact same amount: Δi_{e1} + Δi_{e2} = 0. The total current entering the tail element never changes! Because no AC signal current flows into the tail, the common emitter junction experiences zero AC voltage fluctuation. It behaves as a perfect AC virtual ground, splitting the complex two-transistor circuit into two independent, elementary common-emitter half-circuits.

1. Small-Signal Half-Circuit Derivation

  1. Differential Base Voltages:
    v_{b1} = +[[v_{id}|2]] and v_{b2} = -[[v_{id}|2]]
  2. Virtual Ground at Joined Emitters: Due to anti-symmetry, v_{e} = 0 V AC. Therefore:
    v_{be1} = v_{b1} - v_{e} = +[[v_{id}|2]] and v_{be2} = v_{b2} - v_{e} = -[[v_{id}|2]]
  3. Incremental Collector Currents:
    i_{c1} = g_{m}v_{be1} = g_{m} · [[v_{id}|2]] and i_{c2} = g_{m}v_{be2} = -g_{m} · [[v_{id}|2]]
  4. Collector AC Voltages:
    v_{o1} = -i_{c1}R_{C} = -g_{m}R_{C} · [[v_{id}|2]]
    v_{o2} = -i_{c2}R_{C} = +g_{m}R_{C} · [[v_{id}|2]]
  5. Balanced Differential Output Voltage (v_{od}):
    v_{od} = v_{o1} - v_{o2} = -g_{m}R_{C} · [[v_{id}|2]] - (+g_{m}R_{C} · [[v_{id}|2]]) = -g_{m}R_{C}v_{id}
  6. Balanced Differential Voltage Gain (A_{d,bal}):
    A_{d,bal} = [[v_{od}|v_{id}]] = -g_{m}R_{C} = -[[R_{C}|r_{e}]]
    where r_{e} = V_{T} / I_{E} ≈ 26 mV / I_{C}.

    R_{id} = r_{π1} + r_{π2} = 2r_{π} = 2βr_{e}
  8. Balanced Output Resistance (R_{od}): Looking between Collector 1 and Collector 2:
    R_{od} = R_{C1} + R_{C2} = 2R_{C} (or 2(R_{C} || r_{o}))

Topic 11

Constant-Current Tail Bias and Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

Winter 2025 SDC-II Q5 OR (10 Marks)Common-Mode Half CircuitCMRR in dBActive Tail Source
Intuition · Why Common-Mode Signals See 2R_{E}

When an identical common-mode signal (v_{1} = v_{2} = v_{cm}) strikes both inputs, both transistors try to increase their emitter currents simultaneously. The total current rushing into the tail element is the sum of both: i_{tail} = i_{e1} + i_{e2} = 2i_{e}. In the symmetrical common-mode half-circuit, each transistor's emitter is degenerated not by R_{E}, but by 2R_{E}! This heavy negative feedback crushes the common-mode gain. If we replace the passive resistor R_{E} (~11 kΩ) with an active BJT current mirror having dynamic resistance R_{t} = r_{o} ≈ 1.0 MΩ, common-mode gain plummets from 0.2 down to 0.002, rocketing the CMRR from 46.8 dB to 85.7 dB!

1. Systematic Derivation of Common-Mode Gain (A_{c})

  1. Common-Mode Half-Circuit Model: With v_{b} = v_{cm}, each emitter experiences an effective degenerating resistance of 2R_{E} to ground.
  2. Base Current and Input Loop KVL:
    v_{cm} = i_{b}r_{π} + (1 + β)i_{b}(2R_{E}) = i_{b} · [r_{π} + 2(1 + β)R_{E}]
  3. Single-Ended Common-Mode Collector Output:
    v_{o1,cm} = -i_{c}R_{C} = -β i_{b}R_{C} = -β · [[v_{cm}|r_{π} + 2(1 + β)R_{E}]] · R_{C}
  4. Common-Mode Gain Expression (A_{c}): Dividing by v_{cm}:
    A_{c} = -[[β R_{C}|r_{π} + 2(1 + β)R_{E}]] = -[[R_{C}|r_{e} + 2R_{E} · [[β + 1|β]]]] ≈ -[[R_{C}|r_{e} + 2R_{E}]]
  5. High Tail-Resistance Approximation (2R_{E} >> r_{e}):
    A_{c} ≈ -[[R_{C}|2R_{E}]] (or with current source tail: -[[R_{C}|2R_{t}]])
  6. Ideal Balanced-Output Common-Mode Gain: By perfect symmetry, v_{o1,cm} = v_{o2,cm}. The differential balanced output is:
    v_{od,cm} = v_{o1,cm} - v_{o2,cm} = 0 V ⇒ A_{c,bal} = 0 (ideal CMRR = ∞)
    In practice, slight transistor and resistor mismatches make A_{c,bal} small but non-zero.

2. Derivation of Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

CMRR is defined as the ratio of differential voltage gain magnitude to common-mode voltage gain magnitude:

CMRR = | [[A_{d}|A_{c}]] |

For a single-ended output with resistor tail R_{E}:

CMRR = | [[-[[R_{C}|2r_{e}]]| -[[R_{C}|r_{e} + 2R_{E}]] ]] | = [[r_{e} + 2R_{E}|2r_{e}]] = [[1|2]] + [[R_{E}|r_{e}]] ≈ [[R_{E}|r_{e}]] (since R_{E} >> r_{e})

When biased by a constant-current source with dynamic output resistance R_{t}:

CMRR ≈ [[R_{t}|r_{e}]]

In decibels (dB), CMRR is expressed logarithmically as:

CMRR_{dB} = 20 log_{10}(CMRR) = 20 log_{10}(| [[A_{d}|A_{c}]] |)

3. Four Concrete Methods to Improve CMRR

  1. Replace Passive Resistor with Active Current Mirror Tail: A passive resistor R_{E} is limited to ~10-20 kΩ by DC supply headroom. Replacing it with a BJT current source provides a dynamic impedance R_{t} = r_{o} ≈ 1 MΩ, immediately boosting CMRR by ~40 dB without requiring high DC supply voltages.
  2. Use Wilson or Cascode Current Source Tail: Employing a Wilson mirror (R_{t} ≈ β·r_{o}/2 ≈ 5 MΩ) or cascode mirror increases the dynamic tail resistance into tens of megohms, raising CMRR beyond 100 dB.
  3. Use Balanced (Differential) Output: Common-mode signals produce identical in-phase shifts at both collectors. Measuring the output differentially (v_{o1} - v_{o2}) causes common-mode signals to subtract and cancel out.
  4. Precision Monolithic Transistor Matching: Fabricating transistors Q_{1} and Q_{2} on the same die with cross-coupled quad layout minimizes lithographic and thermal gradients, ensuring Δg_{m} ≈ 0 and ΔR_{C} ≈ 0.

Quick check: Why does CMRR use 20·log10 instead of 10·log10?

In electrical engineering, decibels are defined for power as 10·log10(P_{1}/P_{2}). Because electrical power is proportional to the square of voltage (P = V²/R), the ratio of voltage gains squared becomes 10·log10((A_{d}/A_{c})²) = 2·10·log10(|A_{d}/A_{c}|) = 20·log10(CMRR).

Intuition · Beyond the Small-Signal Approximation

Small-signal analysis (A_{d} = -g_{m}R_{C}) assumes that input voltages are so microscopic that the transistor's exponential curve appears straight. In reality, as differential input voltage v_{id} expands beyond ±20 mV, the differential pair acts as an electronic current steering switch. The total tail current I_{T} is smoothly steered entirely into Q_{1} for positive v_{id}, or entirely into Q_{2} for negative v_{id}. The exact large-signal transfer relationship is governed rigorously by the hyperbolic tangent function: tanh(v_{id}/2V_{T}).

1. Systematic Derivation of the tanh Characteristic

  1. BJT Collector Current Law: Assuming forward-active operation with matched saturation currents I_{S}:
    i_{C1} = I_{S} · e^{v_{BE1}/V_{T}} and i_{C2} = I_{S} · e^{v_{BE2}/V_{T}}
  2. Ratio of Collector Currents:
    [[i_{C1}|i_{C2}]] = e^{(v_{BE1} - v_{BE2})/V_{T}} = e^{v_{id}/V_{T}}
    where v_{id} = v_{b1} - v_{b2} = v_{BE1} - v_{BE2}.
  3. Constant Tail-Current Constraint: Neglecting base currents (α ≈ 1):
    i_{C1} + i_{C2} = I_{T} ⇒ [[i_{C1}|i_{C2}]] + 1 = [[I_{T}|i_{C2}]]
  4. Solving for Individual Collector Currents:
    i_{C2} = [[I_{T}|1 + e^{v_{id}/V_{T}}]] and i_{C1} = [[I_{T} · e^{v_{id}/V_{T}}|1 + e^{v_{id}/V_{T}}]] = [[I_{T}|1 + e^{-v_{id}/V_{T}}]]
  5. Differential Collector Current (i_{C1} - i_{C2}):
    i_{C1} - i_{C2} = I_{T} · [[e^{v_{id}/V_{T}} - 1|e^{v_{id}/V_{T}} + 1]] = I_{T} · [[e^{v_{id}/(2V_{T})} - e^{-v_{id}/(2V_{T})}|e^{v_{id}/(2V_{T})} + e^{-v_{id}/(2V_{T})}]]
    Recognizing the definition of the hyperbolic tangent function tanh(x) = (e^{x} - e^{-x})/(e^{x} + e^{-x}):
    i_{C1} - i_{C2} = I_{T} tanh([[v_{id}|2V_{T}]])
  6. Exact Balanced Differential Output Voltage (v_{od}):
    v_{od} = v_{o1} - v_{o2} = -R_{C}(i_{C1} - i_{C2}) = -I_{T}R_{C} tanh([[v_{id}|2V_{T}]])
  7. Verification of the Small-Signal Linear Limit: Using the Taylor expansion tanh(x) ≈ x - x³/3 for |x| << 1:
    v_{od} ≈ -I_{T}R_{C} · [[v_{id}|2V_{T}]] = -[[I_{T}|2V_{T}]] · R_{C}v_{id} = -g_{m}R_{C}v_{id}
    since g_{m} = (I_{T}/2)/V_{T} at the quiescent balance point! The small-signal formula is simply the first-order tangent of the exact tanh curve at the origin.
Worked Example 12 · Large-Signal Output and Nonlinearity (Dr. Ghongade Notes)

Problem: A differential amplifier has tail current I_{T} = 1.00 mA, collector load resistors R_{C} = 5.0 kΩ, and thermal voltage V_{T} = 26 mV. An input differential voltage v_{id} = 50 mV is applied. Calculate (a) the exact differential output voltage using the large-signal tanh relation, (b) the output voltage predicted by the small-signal linear model, and (c) the percentage overestimation error of the linear model.

Solution:

  1. Dimensionless Argument x = v_{id} / (2V_{T}):
    x = [[v_{id}|2V_{T}]] = [[50 mV|2(26 mV)]] = [[50|52]] ≈ 0.9615
  2. Evaluate Hyperbolic Tangent:
    tanh(0.9615) = [[e^{0.9615} - e^{-0.9615}|e^{0.9615} + e^{-0.9615}]] = [[2.6156 - 0.3823|2.6156 + 0.3823]] = [[2.2333|2.9979]] ≈ 0.7449 ≈ 0.745
  3. Exact Differential Output Voltage:
    v_{od,exact} = -I_{T}R_{C} tanh(0.9615) = -(1.00 mA)(5.0 kΩ)(0.7449) = -(5.0 V)(0.7449) ≈ -3.725 V ≈ -3.73 V
  4. Small-Signal Linear Prediction:
    g_{m} = [[I_{T}/2|V_{T}]] = [[0.50 mA|26 mV]] = 19.23 mS
    A_{d} = -g_{m}R_{C} = -(19.23 mS)(5.0 kΩ) = -96.15
    v_{od,linear} = A_{d} · v_{id} = (-96.15)(50 mV) = -4808 mV ≈ -4.81 V
  5. Percentage Overestimation Error:
    Error % = [[|v_{od,linear}| - |v_{od,exact}|||v_{od,exact}|]] × 100% = [[4.81 - 3.73|3.73]] × 100% = +28.9%

Physical Takeaway: An input of 50 mV causes severe gain compression (+28.9% distortion). The linear small-signal approximation is accurate only for |v_{id}| < ~15 mV.

Problem: A differential amplifier has differential gain A_{d} = 50 and common-mode gain A_{c} = 0.02. The applied input voltages are v_{1} = 102 mV and v_{2} = 98 mV. Calculate (a) differential input v_{id}, (b) common-mode input v_{cm}, (c) total output voltage v_{o}, and (d) the percentage of output voltage representing desired signal vs. common-mode error.

Solution:

  1. Differential Input Signal v_{id}:
    v_{id} = v_{1} - v_{2} = 102 mV - 98 mV = 4 mV
  2. Common-Mode Input Signal v_{cm}:
    v_{cm} = [[v_{1} + v_{2}|2]] = [[102 mV + 98 mV|2]] = 100 mV
  3. Total Output Voltage by Superposition:
    v_{o} = A_{d}v_{id} + A_{c}v_{cm} = (50)(4 mV) + (0.02)(100 mV) = 200 mV + 2 mV = 202 mV
  4. Signal vs. Noise Analysis:

    Desired differential signal component = 200 mV (99.01%).

    Undesired common-mode noise component = 2 mV (0.99%).

    CMRR = [[A_{d}|A_{c}]] = [[50|0.02]] = 2500 ⇒ CMRR_{dB} = 20 log_{10}(2500) ≈ 67.96 dB ≈ 68 dB

Key Insight: At the input, the common-mode interference was 25 times larger than the differential signal (100 mV vs. 4 mV). Thanks to the amplifier's CMRR of 68 dB, the common-mode noise at the output is 100 times smaller than the signal (2 mV vs. 200 mV)!

2. Nine Critical Examination Traps & Student Cautions

Caution · Avoid These Common Exam Mistakes
  1. Balanced vs. Single-Ended Gain: Do not confuse balanced gain (-g_{m}R_{C} = -R_{C}/r_{e}) with single-ended gain (-g_{m}R_{C}/2 = -R_{C}/(2r_{e})). Balanced gain is exactly double!
  2. Tail Resistor in Common-Mode Half-Circuit: Never use R_{E} directly in the common-mode half-circuit. Each transistor conducts half the tail current, so each side sees an effective degenerating resistance of 2R_{E}!
  3. Single-Ended CMRR is Never Infinite: Common-mode cancellation is ideally complete only for a balanced differential output (v_{o1} - v_{o2} = 0). For a single-ended output, common-mode gain A_{c} is finite (~0.2 for resistor tail, ~0.002 for current source tail), so CMRR is finite.
  4. Quiescent Current for r_{e}: When calculating small-signal parameters r_{e} = V_{T}/I_{E} and g_{m} = I_{C}/V_{T}, remember that each transistor carries only half the tail current: I_{C} ≈ I_{T}/2. Using total I_{T} yields r_{e} that is half its true value!
  5. Natural Logarithm in Widlar: In the Widlar equation R_{E} = (V_{T}/I_{O}) ln(I_{ref}/I_{O}), always use the natural logarithm ln, never base-10 log_{10}.
  6. CMRR in Decibels: Always compute CMRR in dB using 20·log10(CMRR), never 10·log10(CMRR).
  7. Compliance Voltage Check: Remember that current sources cease regulating when output voltage falls below compliance headroom: V_{CE(sat)} ≈ 0.2 V for basic mirror, V_{CE(sat)} + I_{O}R_{E} for Widlar, and V_{BE} + V_{CE(sat)} ≈ 0.9 V for Wilson.
  8. Small-Signal Linearity Bounds: Small-signal formulas apply only for |v_{id}| < 15-20 mV. For larger signals, you must use the hyperbolic tangent transfer equation.
  9. Split-Supply Emitter Potential: With bases at 0 V ground, the joined emitter node does not sit at 0 V; it sits at -0.7 V (one diode drop below ground).

Practice Problem 9 · Large-Signal Compression

Problem: An ideal differential pair has I_{T} = 2.0 mA and R_{C} = 3.3 kΩ. Using the exact tanh relation at V_{T} = 26 mV, determine differential output v_{od} for (a) v_{id} = 10 mV and (b) v_{id} = 60 mV.

Practice Problem 10 · Balanced Output Common-Mode Rejection

Problem: Explain why a balanced output ideally rejects common-mode signals completely (v_{od,cm} = 0) even when each collector voltage changes appreciably.

Answer: A common-mode voltage v_{cm} increases both emitter currents equally. Consequently, both collector currents increase by the exact same amount Δi_{C1} = Δi_{C2} = g_{m,cm}v_{cm}. The individual collector voltages both drop by identical amounts: Δv_{C1} = Δv_{C2} = -g_{m,cm}R_{C}v_{cm}. Because the balanced output measures the difference between collectors, v_{od} = v_{C1} - v_{C2} = (V_{C1,0} + Δv_{C1}) - (V_{C2,0} + Δv_{C2}) = 0 V. The common-mode signal is cancelled through differential subtraction.
