Start Here: Core Intuitions of Power Amplifiers

Core FoundationsLarge-Signal PhysicsEfficiency vs Distortion
Intuition 1 · Small Signal vs Large Signal: Why Models Break Down

In small-signal amplifiers (Units 1 to 4), input signals are tiny (a few millivolts). The transistor operates over a microscopic slice of its dynamic characteristics where curves can be treated as perfectly straight lines. Linear models like h-parameters or re work beautifully. But in power amplifiers (large-signal amplifiers), the base voltage swings across the entire active region—from near cut-off right up to saturation. Transistor non-linearities dominate, collector current swings reach several Amperes, and internal power dissipation creates intense heat. Efficiency [[P_{ac}|P_{dc}]] and thermal dissipation become the governing design parameters rather than voltage gain.

Intuition 2 · The Universal Efficiency vs Distortion Trade-off

Every power amplifier is governed by an inescapable physics compromise between conduction angle and efficiency:

  • Class A (θc = 360°): Transistor stays ON during the entire AC cycle. Zero crossover distortion, purest audio reproduction. But because it draws heavy DC quiescent current even when completely idle, theoretical efficiency is capped at 25% (series-fed) or 50% (transformer-coupled). Up to 75% of power is wasted as pure heat.
  • Class B (θc = 180°): Biased exactly at cut-off. Zero quiescent idle current! Theoretical efficiency leaps to 78.5%. But because silicon transistors need 0.7V of base-emitter voltage to turn on, a dead zone appears at every zero crossing—causing harsh crossover distortion.
  • Class AB (180° < θc < 360°): Biased slightly above cut-off (idle current ~5% to 10% of peak). Eliminates the 0.7V dead band while delivering 50% to 70% efficiency. This is the bedrock of modern commercial audio power stages.
  • Class C (θc < 180°): Conducts only in brief pulses (~90°). Extremely high efficiency (~85%), but severe distortion makes it unusable for direct audio. Essential for RF transmitters where resonant LC tank circuits restore sinusoidal purity via flywheel effect.
  • Class D (Switching / PWM): Transistors operate strictly as ON/OFF switches (saturation or cut-off). Because either voltage across the switch is zero or current through it is zero, theoretical internal dissipation is zero! Achieves 90% to 98% efficiency.
Intuition 3 · The Miracle of Push-Pull Symmetry

In single-ended amplifiers, non-linear transfer curves introduce substantial second-harmonic (even-order) distortion. In a push-pull pair, two matched transistors are driven with anti-phase signals (180° apart) into a center-tapped transformer or complementary load. When their collector currents are subtracted in the load winding:
inet = iC1 − iC2 = 2·I1·sin(ωt) + 2·I3·sin(3ωt) + ...
All even harmonics (2nd, 4th, 6th) and DC core-magnetizing currents cancel out identically! Furthermore, power supply ripple is rejected, and core saturation is completely avoided.

Intuition 4 · Impedance Matching & Damping Factor

Loudspeakers have low voice-coil impedances: typically 4 Ω, 8 Ω, or 16 Ω. A standard common-emitter amplifier has an output impedance of several kilo-ohms. Connecting an 8 Ω speaker directly to a 2 kΩ collector resistor would cause 99.6% of signal power to be lost inside the amplifier! Power amplifiers solve this either through an impedance matching transformer with turns ratio [[N_{1}|N_{2}]] = √[[R'_{L}|R_{L}]], or via a complementary emitter follower whose output impedance is naturally tiny (< 0.5 Ω), providing massive damping factor to prevent speaker cone ringing.

Need of Power Amplifiers & System Signal Chain

System Perspective

Why can't a microphone drive a loudspeaker directly? Why do audio systems require multiple cascaded stages? Let's trace the signal journey from acoustic wave to amplified sound.

1.1 Walkthrough of the Signal Chain

  1. Input Transducer (Microphone): Converts acoustic pressure waves into electrical signals. Output voltage is minute—typically few microvolts (µV) to millivolts (mV) (e.g. 1 mV to 10 mV). The source impedance is high (typically 600 Ω to 50 kΩ), and available power is on the order of picowatts (pW).
  2. Small-Signal Voltage Amplifier (Preamplifier): Designed for maximum voltage gain (Av ≈ 100 to 1000) with minimal noise figure. It boosts the delicate mV input up to 1 V to 2 V. However, its collector resistor is relatively large (several kΩ), meaning it cannot supply more than a few milliamperes of current without clipping or collapsing.
  3. Driver / Phase Splitter Stage: Serves as an impedance buffer between the high-gain preamp and the heavy-current power transistors. In push-pull designs, it produces two equal-amplitude, perfectly 180° anti-phase drive signals (v1 and v2 = −v1).
  4. Large-Signal Power Amplifier (Output Stage): Takes the 1V–2V signal and provides massive current gain (Ai >> 1) while preserving voltage swing. Its output impedance is extremely low (< 1 Ω), enabling it to deliver Amperes of peak current and tens to hundreds of Watts into the low speaker impedance without voltage sagging.
  5. Output Transducer (Loudspeaker): Electromagnetic voice coil suspended in a permanent magnet field. Typical voice-coil resistances are 4 Ω, 8 Ω, 16 Ω, or 32 Ω. Converts high-current electrical power into mechanical cone vibrations, projecting acoustic power into the room.

Quick check: Why are h-parameters invalid for designing large-signal power amplifiers?

h-parameters (hie, hfe, hoe, hre) are strictly small-signal differential derivatives evaluated at a static operating point (e.g. hfe = ∂iC/∂iB). In a power amplifier, the operating point swings wildly from cut-off to saturation, where hfe drops drastically, base-emitter resistance changes non-linearly, and junction temperatures rise. Therefore, graphical load line analysis and large-signal parameters must be used.

Amplifier Classification: Conduction Angle & Operating Region

Winter 2025 SDC-II Q3 (10 Marks)Conduction Angle θcEfficiency Spectrum
Intuition

The classification of an amplifier is defined solely by its conduction angle θc—the portion of the 360° input sine-wave cycle during which collector current flows through the active transistor. By shifting the DC Q-point along the dynamic transfer curve from the center of the active region down past cut-off, we trade off harmonic fidelity for electrical efficiency.

2.1 Comprehensive Analysis of Amplifier Classes

  1. Class A Amplifier (θc = 360° or 2π radians):
    • Bias Point: Biased in the center of the linear active region: VCEQ ≈ [[V_{CC}|2]] (series-fed) or VCEQ = VCC (transformer-coupled).
    • Collector Current: Flows continuously for the entire 360° cycle of the input signal (never drops to zero).
    • Output Waveform: Faithful reproduction of input signal with minimal harmonic distortion.
    • Collector Efficiency: Very poor. Maximum theoretical efficiency is 25% for series-fed resistive load and 50% for transformer-coupled load.
    • Thermal Behavior: Heavy idle current dissipation (PQ = VCEQ·ICQ). The transistor runs hottest when NO audio signal is playing!
    • Application: High-fidelity audiophile preamplifiers, low-power RF driver stages, instrumentation amplifiers.
  2. Class B Amplifier (θc = 180° or π radians):
    • Bias Point: Biased precisely at cut-off: ICQ = 0, VBEQ ≈ 0 V.
    • Collector Current: Flows for exactly one half-cycle (180°) of the AC input signal. Transistor is completely turned OFF during the negative half.
    • Output Waveform: A single transistor delivers only half-wave pulses. A push-pull pair (two transistors operating 180° out of phase) is required to reconstruct the complete sine wave.
    • Collector Efficiency: High. Maximum theoretical efficiency is [[π|4]] ≈ 78.54%.
    • Distortion: Suffers from crossover distortion near zero crossings due to the 0.7 V cut-in threshold of silicon BJTs.
    • Thermal Behavior: Zero quiescent power dissipation when signal is absent. Transistor dissipation is proportional to output level.
    • Application: Battery-operated intercoms, public address systems where battery conservation is critical.
  3. Class AB Amplifier (180° < θc < 360°):
    • Bias Point: Biased slightly above cut-off (small quiescent current ICQ ≈ 5% to 10% of peak collector current Ip).
    • Collector Current: Flows for more than half a cycle but less than the full cycle (typically 200° to 220°).
    • Output Waveform: Both transistors conduct simultaneously near the zero crossing, creating a seamless hand-off that completely eliminates crossover distortion.
    • Collector Efficiency: Typically 50% to 70% (theoretical upper limit 78.5%).
    • Advantages: Combines the linearity and purity of Class A with the high efficiency and low idle power of Class B.
    • Application: The universal industry standard for consumer Hi-Fi audio amplifiers, studio monitors, guitar power amps.
  4. Class C Amplifier (θc < 180°, typically 80° to 120°):
    • Bias Point: Biased well below cut-off using reverse base-emitter bias (VBEQ < 0 V).
    • Collector Current: Flows in narrow pulses during the positive peak of the input cycle.
    • Collector Efficiency: Highest among linear-biased circuits, typically 80% to 90%.
    • Distortion: Severe non-linear distortion. Completely unusable for direct audio amplification.
    • Application: Radio Frequency (RF) power transmitters (AM, FM, mobile transmitters). A parallel LC resonant tank circuit forms the collector load, which rings sinusoidally via the "flywheel effect" to reconstruct a pure carrier wave.
  5. Class D Amplifier (Switching / Pulse-Width Modulation):
    • Operating Principle: Non-linear switching amplifier. The audio input is compared against a high-frequency triangular wave (e.g. 250 kHz to 1 MHz) to generate a Pulse-Width Modulated (PWM) square wave.
    • Transistor State: Output MOSFETs operate strictly as rapid electronic switches: either fully ON (saturation, where VDS ≈ 0 V) or fully OFF (cut-off, where ID = 0 A).
    • Efficiency: Since Ploss = VDS · ID is approximately zero in both states, theoretical efficiency approaches 100% (practical commercial circuits achieve 90% to 98%).
    • Reconstruction: A passive low-pass LC Butterworth filter removes the high-frequency switching carrier, delivering the pure amplified audio signal to the loudspeaker.
    • Application: Modern smartphones, automotive audio systems, active subwoofers, ultra-compact portable Bluetooth speakers.

Quick check: Why can't a Class C amplifier be used directly as an audio power amplifier?

In Class C, current flows for only a fraction of the cycle (θc < 180°), clipping more than half of the input audio waveform completely. In RF amplifiers, an LC resonant circuit tuned to the carrier frequency reconstructs the missing portions via electromagnetic oscillation (flywheel effect). In audio, the signal spans three decades of frequencies (20 Hz to 20 kHz), making tuned LC reconstruction impossible; the output would sound intolerably distorted.

Series-Fed Class A Power Amplifier

Winter 2025 Q5 OR (10 Marks)DC Load LineEfficiency Derivation (25%)Figure of Merit = 2
Circuit Concept

The series-fed Class A amplifier is the simplest large-signal topology: the load resistor RL is connected directly in series with the collector of an NPN power BJT. Because current flows continuously through both RL and the transistor, it provides our foundational benchmark for power, dissipation, and efficiency limits.

3.1 Circuit Equations & Load Line Analysis

Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL) to the collector-emitter output loop:

V_{CC} − I_{C} R_{L} − V_{CE} = 0 ⇒ I_{C} = [[V_{CC} − V_{CE}|R_{L}]] = − [[1|R_{L}]] V_{CE} + [[V_{CC}|R_{L}]]

This is the equation of a straight line (the DC Load Line) plotted on the IC versus VCE output characteristics:

  • Cut-off Point (Point A): When transistor is cut off, IC = 0 $⇒$ VCE(cut-off) = VCC. Coordinates: (VCC, 0).
  • Saturation Point (Point B): When transistor saturates, VCE = 0 $⇒$ IC(sat) = [[V_{CC}|R_{L}]]. Coordinates: (0, [[V_{CC}|R_{L}]]).
  • Optimal Q-Point for Maximum Symmetrical Swing: To prevent premature clipping on either peak, the quiescent operating point Q must be located at the precise midpoint of the load line:
V_{CEQ} = [[V_{CC}|2]], I_{CQ} = [[V_{CC}|2 R_{L}]]

3.2 Derivation of Maximum AC Output Power (Pac(max))

When an AC input signal is applied, the operating point swings symmetrically up and down along the load line. Under maximum undistorted excursion:

  • Peak-to-peak output voltage swing: Vopp = VCE(max) − VCE(min) = VCC − 0 = VCC.
  • Peak output voltage: Vp = [[V_{opp}|2]] = [[V_{CC}|2]].
  • RMS output voltage: Vrms = [[V_{p}|√2]] = [[V_{CC}|2√2]].
  • Peak-to-peak output current swing: Iopp = IC(max) − IC(min) = [[V_{CC}|R_{L}]] − 0 = [[V_{CC}|R_{L}]].
  • Peak output current: Ip = [[I_{opp}|2]] = [[V_{CC}|2 R_{L}]].
  • RMS output current: Irms = [[I_{p}|√2]] = [[V_{CC}|2√2 R_{L}]].

The AC power delivered to the load resistor RL is:

P_{ac} = V_{rms} · I_{rms} = [[V_{opp}|2√2]] · [[I_{opp}|2√2]] = [[(V_{max} − V_{min})(I_{max} − I_{min})|8]]

Expressed purely in terms of peak-to-peak voltage across load resistance RL:

P_{ac(max)} = [[V_{opp}^2|8 R_{L}]] = [[(V_{CC})^2|8 R_{L}]] = [[V_{CC}^2|8 R_{L}]]

3.3 Derivation of DC Input Power (Pdc)

The DC power supplied by the battery source VCC is the product of supply voltage and average DC current drawn from it. Because the output current swings sinusoidally around the quiescent bias level ICQ, the average (DC) collector current remains constant at ICQ:

P_{dc} = V_{CC} · I_{CQ} = V_{CC} · [[V_{CC}|2 R_{L}]] = [[V_{CC}^2|2 R_{L}]]

3.4 Derivation of Maximum Collector Efficiency (ηmax)

Collector circuit efficiency η is defined as the ratio of AC output power delivered to the load to the total DC power supplied by the source:

η_{max} = [[P_{ac(max)}|P_{dc}]] × 100% = [[[[V_{CC}^2|8 R_{L}]]|[[V_{CC}^2|2 R_{L}]]]] × 100% = [[2|8]] × 100% = 25%

Physical Conclusion: In a series-fed Class A amplifier, even under optimal, zero-saturation conditions, at most 25% of the DC power supplied is converted into audio output power. The remaining 75% is entirely dissipated as waste heat in the transistor and load resistor.

3.5 Power Dissipation & Figure of Merit (FM)

The power dissipated within the transistor itself is the difference between total DC input power and AC power delivered to the external load:

P_{D} = P_{dc} − P_{ac}
  • Quiescent Condition (Zero Input Signal, Pac = 0):
    P_{Q(max)} = V_{CEQ} · I_{CQ} = [[V_{CC}|2]] · [[V_{CC}|2 R_{L}]] = [[V_{CC}^2|4 R_{L}]]
    The transistor dissipates its maximum possible power when idling! As the audio signal level increases, AC power is delivered to the load, and the internal dissipation of the transistor actually decreases.
  • Figure of Merit (FM): Defined as the ratio of maximum transistor power dissipation to maximum AC output power:
    FM = [[P_{Q(max)}|P_{ac(max)}]] = [[[[V_{CC}^2|4 R_{L}]]|[[V_{CC}^2|8 R_{L}]]]] = 2
    Physical Significance: The transistor must be rated to safely dissipate at least twice the maximum AC power it can deliver to the load (e.g. to produce 10 W of audio power, you must select a transistor rated for at least 20 W of continuous thermal dissipation).

3.6 Practical Efficiency with Saturation Voltage Vce(min)

In a real power transistor, collector voltage cannot drop to zero; it is bounded by the collector saturation voltage VCE(sat) = V_{ce(min)} (typically 0.5 V to 2 V). The practical voltage swing becomes Vopp = VCC − Vce(min), reducing efficiency to:

η = 25% · ([[V_{CC} − V_{ce(min)}|V_{CC}]])^2 or η = [[(V_{max} − V_{min})^2|8 · V_{CC} · V_{CEQ}]] × 100%

Quick check: Why does a Class A amplifier run cooler when playing loud audio than when silent?

The total DC power Pdc drawn from the supply is constant at VCC · ICQ regardless of signal level. Transistor dissipation is PD = Pdc − Pac. When silent (Pac = 0), all DC power is converted to heat inside the transistor (PQ = VCC2 / 4RL). When audio plays at full volume, 25% of that power is transferred to the external speaker as sound and voice-coil heat, leaving only 75% inside the amplifier.

Transformer-Coupled Class A Power Amplifier

Why Use a Transformer?

The series-fed Class A amplifier suffered two crippling weaknesses: (1) low efficiency (25%) because DC current burned power continuously in RL, and (2) inability to drive low-impedance loads (4Ω–16Ω) directly. The transformer-coupled Class A amplifier solves both: the primary winding has near-zero DC resistance (eliminating quiescent DC resistive loss), while its step-down turns ratio transforms the tiny speaker impedance into an optimal large reflected load R'L. Most remarkably, the collector voltage swings all the way up to 2·VCC due to inductive energy storage!

4.1 Reflected Load Impedance (R'L)

Let the output transformer have N1 primary turns and N2 secondary turns, giving turns ratio n = [[N_{1}|N_{2}]]. Across ideal transformer terminals:

[[V_{1}|V_{2}]] = [[N_{1}|N_{2}]] = n ⇒ V_{1} = n V_{2}, [[I_{1}|I_{2}]] = [[N_{2}|N_{1}]] = [[1|n]] ⇒ I_{1} = [[I_{2}|n]]

The effective AC load impedance seen looking into the primary winding is:

R'_{L} = [[V_{1}|I_{1}]] = [[n V_{2}|I_{2} / n]] = n^2 · [[V_{2}|I_{2}]] = ([[N_{1}|N_{2}]])^2 · R_{L}

If primary winding wire has non-zero DC resistance Rpy, the total AC load is R''L = R'L + Rpy.

4.2 DC & AC Load Line Dynamics: Why Voltage Reaches 2·VCC

  1. Vertical DC Load Line: The DC winding resistance of the transformer primary is negligible (Rdc ≈ 0 Ω). Applying KVL under DC conditions:
    V_{CEQ} = V_{CC} − I_{CQ} · R_{dc} ≈ V_{CC}
    The DC load line is a perfectly vertical line at VCE = VCC! Unlike the series-fed stage where VCEQ = VCC/2, the transistor now enjoys the full supply voltage VCC at rest.
  2. AC Load Line: For AC signals, the primary presents impedance R'L. The AC load line passes through the quiescent point Q(VCC, I_{CQ}) with slope −[[1|R'_{L}]].
  3. The Inductive 2·VCC Mechanism: When the input AC signal drives base current downwards toward cut-off (IC drops from ICQ to 0), the magnetic field in the transformer primary core collapses. According to Lenz's Law, the inductor induces a voltage vL = L·[[di|dt]] that opposes the decay of current, effectively adding in series with the battery VCC:
    V_{CE(max)} = V_{CC} + I_{CQ} · R'_{L} = V_{CC} + V_{CC} = 2 · V_{CC}
    The collector-emitter voltage swings symmetrically from 0 V up to 2·VCC!
  4. Symmetrical Current Excursion: When VCE drops to zero during the opposite peak, collector current reaches:
    I_{C(max)} = 2 · I_{CQ} = [[2 V_{CC}|R'_{L}]]

4.3 Derivation of Maximum AC Output Power (Pac(max))

Under full symmetric swing, peak-to-peak output voltage and current are:

V_{opp} = V_{CE(max)} − V_{CE(min)} = 2 V_{CC} − 0 = 2 V_{CC}, I_{opp} = I_{C(max)} − I_{C(min)} = [[2 V_{CC}|R'_{L}]]

The AC power delivered to the transformer primary winding (and transferred to load) is:

P_{ac(max)} = [[V_{opp}^2|8 R'_{L}]] = [[(2 V_{CC})^2|8 R'_{L}]] = [[4 V_{CC}^2|8 R'_{L}]] = [[V_{CC}^2|2 R'_{L}]]

4.4 Derivation of DC Input Power (Pdc)

The average DC current drawn from the battery is simply the constant bias current I_{CQ} = [[V_{CC}|R'_{L}]]:

P_{dc} = V_{CC} · I_{CQ} = V_{CC} · [[V_{CC}|R'_{L}]] = [[V_{CC}^2|R'_{L}]]

4.5 Derivation of Maximum Efficiency (ηmax = 50%)

η_{max} = [[P_{ac(max)}|P_{dc}]] × 100% = [[[[V_{CC}^2|2 R'_{L}]]|[[V_{CC}^2|R'_{L}]]]] × 100% = [[1|2]] × 100% = 50%

Conclusion: By eliminating resistive DC collector loss and doubling voltage swing to 2·VCC, transformer coupling doubles the theoretical efficiency of Class A from 25% to 50%!

4.6 Transistor Dissipation & Figure of Merit

  • Quiescent power dissipation (idle, zero AC signal):
    P_{Q(max)} = V_{CEQ} · I_{CQ} = V_{CC} · [[V_{CC}|R'_{L}]] = [[V_{CC}^2|R'_{L}]]
  • Figure of Merit:
    FM = [[P_{Q(max)}|P_{ac(max)}]] = [[[[V_{CC}^2|R'_{L}]]|[[V_{CC}^2|2 R'_{L}]]]] = 2

Push-Pull Operating Principle

Class A wasted enormous power idling at full bias current. Class B eliminates quiescent current entirely by biasing transistors at cut-off (ICQ = 0). Since one transistor can only conduct for 180° (one half-cycle), we connect two matched transistors in a symmetrical push-pull circuit: one "pushes" current through the load during the positive half-cycle, while the other "pulls" current during the negative half-cycle. The output transformer seamlessly recombines both halves into an amplified replica of the original sine wave!

6.1 Mathematical Proof of Even-Order Harmonic Cancellation

Transistor dynamic transfer characteristics are non-linear, introducing harmonic distortion. Because the two transistors Q1 and Q2 are identical and driven with 180° anti-phase signals, their collector currents can be expanded in Fourier series:

i_{C1} = I_{0} + I_{1} sin(ω t) + I_{2} cos(2ω t) + I_{3} sin(3ω t) + I_{4} cos(4ω t) + …

Since Q2 receives an identical signal delayed by π radians (180°):

i_{C2} = I_{0} + I_{1} sin(ω t − π) + I_{2} cos(2(ω t − π)) + I_{3} sin(3(ω t − π)) + …

Recalling that sin(θ − π) = −sin(θ), cos(2θ − 2π) = +cos(2θ), and sin(3θ − 3π) = −sin(3θ):

i_{C2} = I_{0} − I_{1} sin(ω t) + I_{2} cos(2ω t) − I_{3} sin(3ω t) + I_{4} cos(4ω t) − …

In the center-tapped output transformer primary, current i_{C1} flows downward through the upper winding while i_{C2} flows upward through the lower winding. The net primary magnetizing current inducing voltage in the secondary is the difference between the two:

i_{net} = i_{C1} − i_{C2} = 2 I_{1} sin(ω t) + 2 I_{3} sin(3ω t) + 2 I_{5} sin(5ω t) + …

Profound Conclusions:

  • All even-order harmonic distortion terms (I2, I4, I6) cancel out completely! Second-harmonic distortion, which dominates in single-ended amplifiers, is totally eliminated.
  • All DC bias terms (I0) cancel out identically! Net DC magnetization of the transformer core is zero, preventing core saturation and allowing smaller, lighter magnetic cores.
  • Power supply hum/ripple is cancelled: Any ripple voltage on +VCC enters the center tap and induces equal and opposite magnetic fluxes in both primary halves, cancelling at the secondary.

    P_{ac(max)} = [[1|2]] V_{CC} · I_{p} = [[V_{CC}^2|2 R'_{L}]]

The Root Cause of Crossover Distortion

In theoretical Class B, we assumed transistors turn on the instant base voltage goes positive. In real silicon BJTs, no collector current flows until the base-emitter voltage exceeds the forward cut-in threshold VBE(cut-in) ≈ 0.6 V to 0.7 V. When the input signal swings between −0.7 V and +0.7 V, neither transistor conducts. This creates a flat zero-voltage plateau (dead zone) at every zero crossing of the output waveform called crossover distortion.

7.1 Why Crossover Distortion is Particularly Harmful

  • Harmonic Signature: Crossover notches introduce sharp corners into the waveform, generating high concentrations of high-order odd harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th). Unlike mellow 2nd harmonics, high-order odd harmonics sound metallic, harsh, and grating to the human ear.
  • Inverse Volume Behavior: At loud volumes, the 0.7 V dead band is a negligible fraction of a 30 V swing. But at low listening volumes (e.g. 1 V peak), the 0.7 V dead zone represents 70% of the entire waveform! Thus, crossover distortion becomes worst at low volumes—precisely when listeners expect quiet, clean sound.

7.2 Eliminating Crossover Distortion: Class AB Biasing

To eliminate crossover distortion, we must pre-bias both transistors with a small forward voltage (Vbias ≈ 0.7 V per junction) so that each transistor is idling just on the threshold of conduction. When the input AC signal passes through zero, one transistor seamlessly takes over from the other without any flat dead band.

  1. Diode Biasing in Transformer-Coupled Stages:

    A silicon diode D1 in series with current-limiting resistor R4 is connected between +VCC and the center tap of the input transformer secondary. The 0.7 V drop across forward-biased diode D1 pre-biases both transistor bases slightly positive relative to ground. Each transistor carries a tiny quiescent current ICQ (typically 5% to 10% of peak current Ip).

  2. Thermal Runaway Hazard:

    As power transistors operate, their internal collector junctions heat up. For silicon BJTs, the base-emitter forward voltage drops at a rate of −2 mV/°C with rising temperature:

    [[d V_{BE}|d T]] ≈ − 2 mV/^°C

    If a fixed resistive voltage divider is used for biasing, Vbias remains constant while the transistor requires less V_{BE}. Consequently, quiescent collector current ICQ increases exponentially. Greater current generates more heat, which reduces V_{BE} further, triggering catastrophic thermal runaway and transistor destruction!

  3. Diode Thermal Compensation (Thermal Tracking):

    By mounting the bias diode D1 directly on the same aluminum heatsink as the output power transistors, the diode temperature tracks the transistor temperature. As heatsink temperature rises, the forward drop across D1 drops at the identical rate of −2 mV/°C, automatically reducing the bias voltage applied to the bases. Quiescent current remains perfectly stable across the entire temperature operating range.

Engineering Reality: The PNP Problem

While complementary symmetry looks perfect on paper, in reality high-power PNP transistors were historically difficult, expensive, and fragile compared to robust silicon NPN devices (like the legendary 2N3055). To solve this, H.C. Lin invented the Quasi-Complementary Symmetry topology: using low-power complementary driver transistors to control two identical, rugged, high-power NPN output transistors!

11.1 Quasi-Complementary Symmetry (Lin Topology)

The quasi-complementary output configuration splits into two composite transistor pairs:

  • Upper Push Branch (Darlington Pair): Consists of an NPN driver transistor (Q2) feeding an NPN power output transistor (Q4).
    Effective Current Gain: βeff ≈ β2 · β4.
    Base-to-Output Threshold: VBE(total) = VBE2 + VBE4 ≈ 0.7 V + 0.7 V = 1.4 V.
  • Lower Pull Branch (Sziklai Pair / Complementary Feedback Pair CFP): Consists of a low-power PNP driver transistor (Q3) feeding an NPN power output transistor (Q5).
    Effective Operation: The combination acts externally as an equivalent high-power PNP transistor, but the heavy collector current flows through the rugged NPN device Q5!
    Base-to-Output Threshold: Only ONE forward base-emitter drop: Vin = VEB3 ≈ 0.7 V.
  • Asymmetry & Modern Solution: Because the upper Darlington requires 1.4 V while the lower Sziklai requires 0.7 V, a small diode or asymmetric resistor string is used to ensure identical turn-on thresholds, eliminating crossover notches. Both power output transistors connected to the chassis heatsink are identical NPN types!

11.2 Harmonic Distortion & Mathematical Analysis (THD)

When large-signal voltage swings drive a transistor into non-linear transfer characteristics, the output collector current deviates from a pure sine wave, producing harmonic distortion:

i_{C} = I_{CQ} + I_{0} + I_{1} cos(ω t) + I_{2} cos(2ω t) + I_{3} cos(3ω t) + I_{4} cos(4ω t) + …

where I1 is the fundamental audio amplitude, and I2, I3, I4... are harmonic distortion amplitudes.

Three-Point Harmonic Analysis (Second Harmonic Distortion D2)

For single-ended amplifiers where second-harmonic distortion dominates, evaluate collector currents at three key angles: ωt = 0° (peak), ωt = 90° (quiescent), and ωt = 180° (trough):

  • At ωt = 0°: i_{C} = I_{max} = I_{CQ} + I_{0} + I_{1} + I_{2}
  • At ωt = 90°: i_{C} = I_{CQ}
  • At ωt = 180°: i_{C} = I_{min} = I_{CQ} + I_{0} − I_{1} + I_{2}

Solving these simultaneous equations yields the harmonic amplitudes:

I_{0} = I_{2} = [[I_{max} + I_{min} − 2 I_{CQ}|4]], I_{1} = [[I_{max} − I_{min}|2]]

The Second Harmonic Distortion (D2) is:

D_{2} = |[[I_{2}|I_{1}]]| = |[[I_{max} + I_{min} − 2 I_{CQ}|2(I_{max} − I_{min})]]| × 100%

Total Harmonic Distortion (THD) Definition

When higher-order harmonics are present (using 5-point analysis), Total Harmonic Distortion represents the combined RMS value of all unwanted harmonics relative to the fundamental:

THD = √D_{2}^2 + D_{3}^2 + D_{4}^2 + D_{5}^2 + … × 100% = √([[I_{2}|I_{1}]])^2 + ([[I_{3}|I_{1}]])^2 + ([[I_{4}|I_{1}]])^2 + … × 100%

The total AC power delivered to the load including harmonic contamination is:

P_{total} = P_{1} (1 + D_{2}^2 + D_{3}^2 + D_{4}^2 + …) = P_{1} (1 + THD^2)

where P1 = (1/2)·I12·RL is the true fundamental audio power.

11.4 Thermal Physics & Heatsink Design

The heat generated at the semiconductor collector-base junction must travel through the transistor casing and mounting hardware into the ambient air. This thermal flow obeys an exact electrical Ohm's Law analogy:

T_{J} − T_{A} = P_{D} · θ_{JA} = P_{D} · (θ_{JC} + θ_{CS} + θ_{SA})
  • TJ = Maximum safe semiconductor junction temperature (typically 150°C to 200°C for Silicon).
  • TA = Maximum expected ambient air temperature (typically 25°C to 50°C).
  • PD = Power dissipated by the transistor as heat (Watts).
  • θJC = Thermal resistance from junction to transistor case (°C/W, given by manufacturer datasheet).
  • θCS = Thermal resistance across mica insulating washer and thermal silicone grease (°C/W, typically 0.5 °C/W).
  • θSA = Thermal resistance of the aluminum finned heatsink to ambient air (°C/W).

To ensure the transistor never exceeds its safe junction temperature, the maximum allowable heatsink thermal resistance is calculated as:

θ_{SA(max)} = [[T_{J(max)} − T_{A}|P_{D}]] − (θ_{JC} + θ_{CS})

Model Solution 1 · Summer 2026 Q5 (a) [10 Marks]

Question: What are the advantages of Class B amplifier over Class A amplifiers? Using suitable equations and diagrams, discuss the efficiency of Class B amplifiers.

Answer Architecture:

  1. Advantages of Class B over Class A (3 marks):
    • Higher Efficiency: Class B reaches a maximum theoretical efficiency of 78.54% (practical 65–70%), compared to only 25% for series-fed Class A and 50% for transformer-coupled Class A.
    • Zero Quiescent Dissipation: Transistors are biased at cut-off (ICQ = 0). When no audio signal is present, the amplifier draws negligible DC power, eliminating standby battery drain.
    • Smaller Heatsinks & Device Economy: Transistor power dissipation is shared between two devices, peaking at only ~20% of VCC2/R'L at 63.6% swing, allowing much smaller heatsinks.
    • Even Harmonic Cancellation: Push-pull operation cancels 2nd, 4th, and all even harmonics, drastically reducing harmonic distortion.
  2. Circuit Diagram & Operation (2 marks):

    Draw the center-tapped transformer-coupled Class B push-pull schematic (Figure 6.1). Show that input transformer T1 applies anti-phase signals v1 and v2 = −v1. Transistor Q1 conducts for positive 180°, and Q2 conducts for negative 180°.

  3. Collector Circuit Efficiency Derivation (5 marks):

    Step 1: DC Input Power (Pdc): Supply current is a full-wave rectified sine wave drawn by both transistors:

    I_{dc} = [[2|π]] · I_{p} ⇒ P_{dc} = V_{CC} · I_{dc} = [[2|π]] V_{CC} · I_{p}

    Step 2: AC Output Power (Pac): Peak voltage across each half of the primary is Vp ≈ VCC:

    P_{ac(max)} = [[V_{rms}^2|R'_{L}]] = [[(V_{p}/√2)^2|R'_{L}]] = [[1|2]] V_{p} I_{p} = [[1|2]] V_{CC} I_{p}

    Step 3: Maximum Efficiency:

    η_{max} = [[P_{ac(max)}|P_{dc}]] × 100% = [[[[1|2]] V_{CC} I_{p}|[[2|π]] V_{CC} I_{p}]] × 100% = [[π|4]] × 100% = 78.54%
Model Solution 2 · Summer 2026 Q5 (a) OR [10 Marks Numerical]

Question: In a Class B amplifier, Vce(min) = 2V and VCC = 15V. Find the collector circuit efficiency.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Given Parameters:
    Supply Voltage VCC = 15 V
    Minimum collector saturation voltage Vce(min) = 2 V
  2. Determine Peak Output Voltage Swing (Vp):

    Because the collector voltage cannot drop below Vce(min) = 2 V, the maximum peak AC voltage swing across the primary load is:

    V_{p} = V_{CC} − V_{ce(min)} = 15 V − 2 V = 13 V
  3. Formulate AC Output Power (Pac):
    P_{ac} = [[1|2]] V_{p} · I_{p} = [[1|2]] × (13 V) · I_{p} = 6.5 · I_{p} Watts
  4. Formulate DC Input Power (Pdc):

    The DC power drawn from the 15 V supply depends on supply voltage and average current:

    P_{dc} = V_{CC} · I_{dc} = V_{CC} · ([[2|π]] I_{p}) = 15 V × [[2|π]] · I_{p} = [[30|π]] · I_{p} ≈ 9.5493 · I_{p} Watts
  5. Calculate Collector Circuit Efficiency (η):
    η = [[P_{ac}|P_{dc}]] × 100% = [[6.5 · I_{p}|9.5493 · I_{p}]] × 100% = 68.07%

    Alternative Verification via Generalized Formula:

    η = [[π|4]] · ([[V_{CC} − V_{ce(min)}|V_{CC}]]) × 100% = 78.54% × ([[13|15]]) = 78.54% × 0.8667 = 68.07%

    Final Answer: The collector circuit efficiency is 68.07%.

Model Solution 4 · Winter 2025 Q5 (a) OR [10 Marks Numerical]

Question: In a Class A amplifier, Vce(max) = 15V, Vce(min) = 1V. Find overall efficiency for (i) series-fed load, (ii) transformer coupled load.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Common Given Data: Vce(max) = 15 V, Vce(min) = 1 V.
Peak-to-peak voltage swing: Vopp = Vce(max) − Vce(min) = 15 V − 1 V = 14 V.

  1. Part (i): Series-Fed Resistive Load:
    • In a series-fed Class A amplifier, the maximum collector voltage cannot exceed the DC supply voltage:
      V_{CC} = V_{ce(max)} = 15 V
    • The quiescent operating point is located midway between saturation and cut-off:
      V_{CEQ} = [[V_{ce(max)} + V_{ce(min)}|2]] = [[15 V + 1 V|2]] = 8 V
    • The AC output power delivered to load resistor RL is:
      P_{ac} = [[(V_{ce(max)} − V_{ce(min)})^2|8 R_{L}]] = [[(14 V)^2|8 R_{L}]] = [[196|8 R_{L}]] = [[24.5|R_{L}]]
    • The quiescent collector current is:
      I_{CQ} = [[V_{CC} − V_{CEQ}|R_{L}]] = [[15 V − 8 V|R_{L}]] = [[7 V|R_{L}]]
    • The DC power drawn from the supply is:
      P_{dc} = V_{CC} · I_{CQ} = 15 V × [[7 V|R_{L}]] = [[105|R_{L}]]
    • Overall Efficiency for Series-Fed Load:
      η_{series} = [[P_{ac}|P_{dc}]] × 100% = [[[[24.5|R_{L}]]|[[105|R_{L}]]]] × 100% = [[24.5|105]] × 100% = 23.33%

      (Formula verification: η = 25% · ((15−1)/(15+1)) = 25% · (14/15) · (7/8) = 23.33%).

  2. Part (ii): Transformer-Coupled Load:
    • In a transformer-coupled amplifier, the DC load line is vertical at VCC (since winding DC resistance is negligible). Therefore, the supply voltage equals the quiescent voltage:
      V_{CC} = V_{CEQ} = [[V_{ce(max)} + V_{ce(min)}|2]] = [[15 V + 1 V|2]] = 8 V
    • The peak AC voltage swing across the primary reflected load R'L is:
      V_{p} = [[V_{ce(max)} − V_{ce(min)}|2]] = [[15 V − 1 V|2]] = 7 V
    • The AC power delivered to the reflected load is:
      P_{ac} = [[V_{p}^2|2 R'_{L}]] = [[(7 V)^2|2 R'_{L}]] = [[49|2 R'_{L}]] = [[24.5|R'_{L}]]
    • The quiescent current required to sustain this swing without clipping is:
      I_{CQ} = [[V_{p}|R'_{L}]] = [[7 V|R'_{L}]]
    • The DC power drawn from the 8V supply is:
      P_{dc} = V_{CC} · I_{CQ} = 8 V × [[7 V|R'_{L}]] = [[56|R'_{L}]]
    • Overall Efficiency for Transformer-Coupled Load:
      η_{xfmr} = [[P_{ac}|P_{dc}]] × 100% = [[[[24.5|R'_{L}]]|[[56|R'_{L}]]]] × 100% = [[24.5|56]] × 100% = 43.75%

      (Formula verification: η = 50% · [[V_{max} − V_{min}|V_{max} + V_{min}]] = 50% · [[14|16]] = 50% · 0.875 = 43.75%).

Summary of Results:
(i) Series-Fed Efficiency = 23.33%
(ii) Transformer-Coupled Efficiency = 43.75%
