Five ideas behind everything in this unit

If these five are clear, the rest of the unit is just applying them again and again.

Idea 1 · The valve

A BJT is a current-controlled valve. A tiny base current IB lets a much bigger collector current IC flow: IC = β·IB. Think of a water tap: a small turn of the handle (base) changes a big flow of water (collector). An amplifier is just that valve plus a resistor RC that turns the changing current into a changing voltage.

Idea 2 · DC and AC live together

The battery (DC) puts the transistor at a resting point called the Q-point. The signal (AC) is a small wobble around that point. So every voltage is “DC part + AC part”:

v_{BE} = V_{BE} + v_{be}    i_{C} = I_{C} + i_{c}    v_{CE} = V_{CE} + v_{ce}

We analyse the two parts separately (superposition). Topic 1 and Topic 10 are about the DC / big picture. Topics 2–9 are about the AC wobble.

Idea 3 · Small wobble = straight line

The real relation IC = ISeVBE/VT is a curve. But zoom in on a tiny piece around the Q-point and a curve looks like a straight line. A straight-line device can be replaced by simple resistors and controlled sources. That replacement is the small-signal model (Topic 3). The widget in Topic 3 lets you watch what happens when the wobble stops being small.

Idea 4 · What AC sees

For the AC signal the circuit looks different from what the battery sees. Four steps to draw the AC equivalent circuit:

  1. DC sources → ground. VCC is a constant, it has no wobble, so for AC it is the same as ground. RC and R1 that connected to VCC now connect to ground.
  2. Capacitors → wires (short circuit) at signal frequencies. Coupling capacitors C1, C3 and the bypass capacitor C2 have almost zero impedance there (XC = 1/2πfC).
  3. Replace the transistor by its small-signal model (h-parameter, hybrid-π or T).
  4. Solve with ordinary circuit laws to get Zi, Zo, Av, Ai, Ap.

Topic 1

Concept of the AC load line

Asked: Summer 2026 Q1(a) — 10 marksPrerequisite for amplifier design and distortion

1.1 What is a load line, and why do we need two?

Intuition

The output characteristic is a map of every (VCE, IC) the transistor could be at. The circuit decides which points it is allowed to be at. That allowed path is the load line. It is the road the operating point drives along.

With only DC, the road is set by VCC and the total resistance in the collector–emitter loop (RC + RE). That is the DC load line. The Q-point sits on it.

When a signal arrives, the capacitors change the picture. The bypass capacitor shorts RE for AC, and the coupling capacitor adds the load RL in parallel with RC for AC. The transistor now “sees” a smaller resistance, so the operating point moves along a different, steeper road: the AC load line. At zero signal the point is still the Q-point, so the AC load line always passes through Q.

Purpose of the AC load line (write these in the exam):

  • It shows the real swing of iC and vCE when a signal is applied. The DC load line cannot show this.
  • It gives the maximum undistorted output swing (how big the output can be before clipping).
  • It tells where to put the Q-point for symmetrical clipping: at the middle of the AC load line.
  • It is used in the design of amplifiers and to check for distortion.

1.2 Drawing both lines

DC load line (Kirchhoff on the collector–emitter loop):

V_{CE} = V_{CC} − I_{C}(R_{C} + R_{E})

Two points are enough: cutoff point A = (VCC, 0) and saturation point B = (0, VCC/(RC+RE)). Q is found from the bias: VB = VCCR2/(R1+R2), then IE = (VB − 0.7)/RE ≈ IC.

AC load line. For a small change, the change in vCE is the change in iC times the AC resistance rac the transistor drives:

Δv_{CE} = −Δi_{C} · r_{ac}  ⇒  V_{CE} = V_{CEQ} − (I_{C} − I_{CQ}) · r_{ac}

That is a straight line with slope −1/rac through Q. Its intercepts:

I_{C(max)} = I_{CQ} + [[V_{CEQ}|r_{ac}]]     V_{CE(max)} = V_{CEQ} + I_{CQ} · r_{ac}

Common mistake

Writing rac = RC + RE when CE is present. The bypass capacitor removes RE for AC. And remember the load RL only enters when it is coupled through a capacitor; a direct-coupled load also changes the DC line.

1.4 Maximum undistorted swing and where to put Q

The AC load line meets the VCE axis at VCE(max) (transistor cut off) and the IC axis at IC(max) (transistor saturated, VCE ≈ 0). The signal can swing from Q towards either end. Whichever end is closer clips first.

V_{p(max)} = min( I_{CQ} · r_{ac} , V_{CEQ} )    V_{pp(max)} = 2 V_{p(max)}
  • If ICQ·rac < VCEQ: output clips on the cutoff side first (Q too low).
  • If ICQ·rac > VCEQ: output clips on the saturation side first (Q too high).
  • Best: ICQ·rac = VCEQ. Q at the centre of the AC load line.

1.5 Worked example (from your notes)

VCC = 20 V, RC = 2.2 kΩ, RE = 2.7 kΩ, R1 = 18 kΩ, R2 = 8.2 kΩ. RE is bypassed by CE.

  1. DC load line. Total DC load = 2.2 + 2.7 = 4.9 kΩ. Point A = (20 V, 0). Point B = (0, 20/4.9 k) = (0, 4.08 mA).
  2. Q-point. Assume IB ≪ I2. VB = 20 × 8.2k/(18k+8.2k) ≈ 6.3 V. VE = 6.3 − 0.7 = 5.6 V. IC ≈ IE = 5.6/2.7k = 2.07 mA. VCEQ = 20 − 2.07 mA × 4.9k = 9.86 V.
  3. AC load line (no external load). rac = RC = 2.2 kΩ. Take ΔIC = 1 mA, then ΔVCE = −1 mA × 2.2k = −2.2 V. That gives point C = (9.86 − 2.2, 2.07 + 1) = (7.66 V, 3.07 mA). Draw the line through Q and C (this is the method in your notes). Intercepts: VCE(max) = 9.86 + 2.07 × 2.2 = 14.4 V, IC(max) = 2.07 + 9.86/2.2k = 6.55 mA.
  4. If a 4.7 kΩ load is coupled. rac = 2.2k ∥ 4.7k = 1.5 kΩ. Intercepts: VCE(max) = 12.96 V, IC(max) = 8.65 mA. The line is steeper still.
  5. Swing. With RL = 4.7k: ICQ·rac = 3.10 V against VCEQ = 9.86 V, so it clips on the cutoff side. Vp(max) = 3.1 V, Vpp ≈ 6.2 V.
10-mark answer skeleton

  2. Circuit with C1, C2 (CE), C3, RL. DC load line equation and intercepts (2).
  3. AC load line: equation, slope −1/rac, passes through Q, intercepts. Neat graph with both lines (3).
  4. Comparison table: bypassed RE → rac = RC∥RL; unbypassed → (RC∥RL)+RE; capacitor-coupled vs direct-coupled load (2).

Quick check 1: RC = 3 kΩ, RL = 6 kΩ, RE bypassed. What is rac?

rac = 3k ∥ 6k = 2 kΩ. The DC load would be RC + RE, which is bigger, so the AC line is steeper.

Quick check 2: Why must the AC load line pass through Q?

With zero signal the transistor is exactly at its DC operating point, and that point satisfies both the DC and the AC relationship. The signal moves it away from Q along the AC line.

Topic 2

BJT as a two-port network

Foundation for all small-signal analysisAsked with: h-parameter equivalent circuit (Summer 2026 Q1 OR)

2.1 Why “two-port”?

Intuition

An amplifier takes a signal in at one pair of terminals and delivers a bigger signal out at another pair. A BJT has only three terminals, so one terminal is shared (common) between input and output: CE, CB or CC. Now we have an input port (2 terminals) and an output port (2 terminals): a two-port network.

We then stop caring what is inside the box. We only describe how the four quantities (v1, i1 at the input, v2, i2 at the output) depend on each other.

2.2 Why the h (hybrid) set?

Four variables, two equations: pick two as independent, the other two follow. Different choices give z, y, h and ABCD parameters. For transistors, the h-parameters win, because:

  • The BJT input is current-driven (IB controls everything) and the output is best described by its voltage (VCE). h takes I1 and V2 as independent variables. That matches.
  • They are measured with short-circuit at the output (easy for AC: just a big capacitor) and open-circuit at the input (easy: a constant-current bias). z-parameters need open outputs and y-parameters need shorted inputs, which are hard to get on a biased transistor, especially at high frequency.
  • They are listed on data sheets.

2.3 The h-parameter equations

v_{1} = h_{11} i_{1} + h_{12} v_{2}     i_{2} = h_{21} i_{1} + h_{22} v_{2}

Switch off one variable at a time to see what each parameter means:

Letter code: i = input, r = reverse, f = forward, o = output. A third letter says which configuration: e, b or c. So for common emitter:

Each equation is a circuit law, so each draws as a circuit. The first is a KVL on the input loop: a resistor hie in series with a voltage source hrevce. The second is a KCL at the output node: a current source hfeib in parallel with a conductance hoe (a resistor 1/hoe).

Topic 3

BJT small-signal models: h-parameter, hybrid-π, T and Ebers-Moll

Asked: Winter 2025 Q1(a) — significance + Ebers-Moll diagramFour models, four jobs

3.0 Why do we need small-signal models at all?

Intuition

A transistor is non-linear. Non-linear circuits cannot be solved with simple network theorems. But for a tiny signal around the Q-point the curve is practically a straight line, so the transistor behaves like a linear circuit made of resistors and dependent sources. Then we can use KVL, KCL, superposition and Thevenin.

Significance (use as bullet points in the exam):

  • Converts a non-linear device into a linear equivalent, so ordinary circuit analysis works.
  • Gives Zi, Zo, Av, Ai, Ap in closed form.
  • Every model parameter depends on the Q-point (IC), so DC bias and AC behaviour are linked.
  • Valid only for small signals, with no clipping, around Q.
  • Different models suit different frequency ranges and viewpoints.

3.1 Model 1: Hybrid (h-parameter)

Already built in Topic 2: hie, hre, hfe, hoe. Parameters come from measurement (data sheet or lab). Good at low and mid frequencies. The usual choice for exam analysis of CE, CC and CB amplifiers.

3.2 Model 2: Hybrid-π

Intuition

Hybrid-π is built from physics. Each element is a derivative of the transistor equations at the Q-point. It is the most popular model for high-frequency analysis because you can add the junction capacitances.

Where the elements come from (a favourite derivation):

  1. Transconductance gm. iC = ISevBE/VT. Differentiate with respect to vBE: gm = ∂iC/∂vBE = IC/VT. This is the slope of the curve at Q, the “straight line” of Idea 3.
  2. Input resistance rπ. iB = iC/β, so rπ = ∂vBE/∂iB = β/gm = βVT/IC.
  3. Output resistance ro. The Early effect makes the output curves slope up: iC ∝ (1 + vCE/VA). So ro = ∂vCE/∂iC ≈ VA/IC.
g_{m} = [[I_{C}|V_{T}]]    r_{π} = [[βV_{T}|I_{C}]] = [[β|g_{m}]]    r_{o} = [[V_{A}|I_{C}]]    V_{T} ≈ 25 mV at room temperature

VA is the Early voltage (50–200 V typical). Note: your design example uses VT = 26 mV, which is T/11600 at 27 °C.

Link to h-parameters: hie ≈ rπ, hfe = β = gmrπ, hoe ≈ 1/ro, hre ≈ 0. That also gives the handy formula hie = hfeVT/IC used in your design example when the data sheet does not list hie.

High-frequency elements. Cπ (base-emitter: diffusion + junction capacitance) and Cμ (base-collector junction capacitance). They make the gain fall at high frequency (Topic 8).

3.3 Model 3: T-model (re model)

Intuition

The T-model looks at the transistor from the emitter. Looking into the emitter you see a small resistor re = VT/IE. Looking into the base you see the same re but multiplied by (β+1), because the base current is (β+1) times smaller than the emitter current. This one rule explains many results later: a resistor in the emitter looks (β+1) times bigger from the base.

r_{e} = [[v_{be}|i_{e}]] = [[V_{T}|I_{E}]]    α = [[β|β+1]] ≈ 0.98 to 0.995    r_{π} = (β+1) r_{e}

Proof of the last relation: vbe = iere = (β+1)ibre, so vbe/ib = (β+1)re = rπ. Hybrid-π and T-model are the same transistor drawn from two viewpoints. The T-model is simple and intuitive for low-frequency work.

3.4 Model 4: Ebers-Moll

Intuition

A BJT is two PN diodes back to back, but the two junctions talk to each other: electrons injected from the emitter cross the thin base and reach the collector. Ebers-Moll captures this with two diodes plus two current sources. It is a large-signal model that works in every region (active, saturation, cutoff). Small-signal models are obtained from it by differentiating at the Q-point.

Identify all currents and voltages (the exam asks for this):

SymbolMeaning
IE, IB, ICTerminal currents of emitter, base, collector
VBE, VBCBase-emitter and base-collector junction voltages
IF = IES(eVBE/VT − 1)Forward diode current of the emitter junction DE
IR = ICS(eVBC/VT − 1)Reverse diode current of the collector junction DC
αFIFPart of the forward current that reaches the collector (αF ≈ 0.98–0.995)
αRIRPart of the reverse current that reaches the emitter (αR is much smaller than αF)

I_{E} = I_{ES}(e^{V_{BE}/V_{T}} − 1) − α_{R} I_{CS}(e^{V_{BC}/V_{T}} − 1)
I_{C} = α_{F} I_{ES}(e^{V_{BE}/V_{T}} − 1) − I_{CS}(e^{V_{BC}/V_{T}} − 1)
I_{B} = (1−α_{F}) I_{ES}(e^{V_{BE}/V_{T}} − 1) + (1−α_{R}) I_{CS}(e^{V_{BC}/V_{T}} − 1)

Equivalent short form: IE = IF − αRIR, IC = αFIF − IR, and IB = IE − IC. Reciprocity: αFIES = αRICS = IS.

In the active region (VBE forward, VBC reverse by several VT), the IR terms collapse to a constant and IC ≈ αFIESeVBE/VT. That is the exponential law used everywhere above.

Topic 4

Analysis of the CE amplifier: Zi, Zo, Av, Ai, Ap

Asked: Winter 2025 Q1 OR (numeric) · SDC-I Winter 2025 Q3 OR (derivation) · Summer 2026 Q1 OR (draw h-equivalent)Most-repeated topic in the papers

4.1 The circuit and the job of every component

PartWhat it does
R1, R2Voltage divider that fixes the base voltage VB, so the DC bias does not depend much on β.
RCTurns the collector current into an output voltage. Sets the gain together with RL.
REStabilises the Q-point (emitter-current bias). If IC rises, VE rises, VBE falls, and IC is pulled back.
C1 (input coupling)Passes the AC signal from the source, blocks DC. Without it the source resistance rs would disturb the bias.
C2 (bypass, “CE”)Short-circuits RE for AC only. DC still sees RE (stability), AC does not (full gain). This is called AC bypassing.
C3 (output coupling)Passes the amplified AC to the load RL, blocks the DC at the collector.

Polarity matters for electrolytic capacitors: the negative terminal must always sit at a DC voltage lower than or equal to the positive terminal.

4.2 What actually happens (step by step)

Intuition
  1. Source voltage goes up a little → vBE rises → base current rises.
  2. Collector current rises β times as much (ic = gmvbe).
  3. Bigger current through RC means a bigger voltage drop across RC.
  4. Collector voltage = VCC − drop, so it goes down. The output moves opposite to the input: 180° phase shift. That is the minus sign in Av.

Numbers: IC = 1 mA gives gm = 40 mS. An input wobble of +1 mV gives ic = 40 µA. Across an AC load of 4 kΩ that is 0.16 V, so vo = −0.16 V. Gain = −160.

4.4 Derivations

Assumptions (state them first in the exam): hreVo is negligible (hre ~ 10−4); 1/hoe ≫ RC, so hoe is ignored in the gain; capacitors are shorts.

(a) Input impedance

Bypassed RE: the input loop is vi = ibhie, so

Z_{b} = h_{ie}

Unbypassed RE: the emitter current (ib + ic) flows through RE.

v_{i} = i_{b}h_{ie} + i_{e}R_{E} = i_{b}h_{ie} + R_{E}(i_{b} + i_{c}) = i_{b}h_{ie} + R_{E}(1 + h_{fe}) i_{b}
⇒ Z_{b} = [[v_{i}|i_{b}]] = h_{ie} + (1+h_{fe}) R_{E}

RE appears (1+hfe) times larger at the base: this is the “emitter resistor reflected into the base” rule of the T-model. The whole circuit sees the bias resistors in parallel:

Z_{i} = R_{1} ∥ R_{2} ∥ Z_{b}

(b) Output impedance

Looking into the collector–emitter terminals the transistor shows a large resistance 1/hoe. RC is in parallel with it (RL is the load, not part of Zo):

Z_{o} = (1/h_{oe}) ∥ R_{C} ≈ R_{C}

(c) Voltage gain

v_{o} = −i_{c}(R_{C} ∥ R_{L})    v_{i} = i_{b}h_{ie}    i_{c} = h_{fe}i_{b}
A_{v} = [[v_{o}|v_{i}]] = −[[h_{fe}(R_{C}∥R_{L})|h_{ie}]]
A_{v} = −[[h_{fe}(R_{C}∥R_{L})|h_{ie}]]  (bypassed)

The minus sign means 180° phase shift. For unbypassed RE replace hie by Zb:

A_{v} = −[[h_{fe}(R_{C}∥R_{L})|h_{ie} + (1+h_{fe})R_{E}]] ≈ −[[R_{C}∥R_{L}|R_{E}]]

The approximation holds when (1+hfe)RE ≫ hie. Gain now depends only on resistor ratios, not on β. This is the price (lower gain) and reward (stable gain) of negative feedback from an unbypassed RE. Partial bypass gives a gain in between.

(d) Current gain

The transistor itself: AiT = Ic/Ib = hfe. For the whole circuit, the source current is splits between RB = R1∥R2 and Zb (current divider), then ic splits between RC and RL:

i_{b} = [[i_{s}R_{B}|R_{B}+Z_{b}]]   i_{c} = [[h_{fe}i_{s}R_{B}|R_{B}+Z_{b}]]   i_{L} = [[i_{c}R_{C}|R_{C}+R_{L}]]
A_{i} = [[i_{L}|i_{s}]] = [[h_{fe}R_{C}R_{B}|(R_{C}+R_{L})(R_{B}+Z_{b})]]

If no RL is connected: Ic/Is = hfeRB/(RB+Zb). In every case the circuit gain is smaller than hfe, because some current is lost in the bias resistors and in RC.

(e) Power gain

Transistor: A_{pT} = [[v_{o}i_{c}|v_{i}i_{b}]] = A_{v} × h_{fe}     Circuit: A_{p} = A_{v} × A_{i}

Topic 5

Analysis of the CC amplifier (emitter follower)

Likely asked as derivation of Zi, Zo, Av, Ai, Ap

5.1 Circuit and intuition

Intuition

The emitter always sits about 0.7 V below the base: vE = vB − 0.7. So if the base moves up 1 V, the emitter follows up by almost 1 V. That is why it is called the emitter follower, and why Av ≈ 1 with no phase change.

It is 100 % negative feedback: all of vo returns to the input (hrc ≈ 1). Feedback this strong gives a high input impedance and a very low output impedance. So the CC stage is a buffer: it lets a weak source (high impedance) drive a heavy load (low impedance) without losing voltage.

5.2 Derivations

(a) Input impedance. hrc ≈ 1, so all of vo is fed back into the input loop:

v_{i} = i_{b}h_{ic} + v_{o} = i_{b}h_{ic} + i_{e}(R_{E}∥R_{L}) = i_{b}[h_{ic} + h_{fc}(R_{E}∥R_{L})]
Z_{b} = h_{ic} + h_{fc}(R_{E} ∥ R_{L})    Z_{i} = R_{1} ∥ R_{2} ∥ Z_{b}

The load (RE∥RL) is multiplied by (1+hfe): that is why Zi is so large.

(b) Output impedance. Switch the signal source off (vs = 0), apply vo at the emitter and find the current it draws. The base current is produced by vo through hic in series with the source-side resistance R1∥R2∥rs:

i_{b} = [[v_{o}|h_{ic} + (R_{1}∥R_{2}∥r_{s})]]   i_{e} = h_{fc}i_{b}
Z_{e} = [[v_{o}|i_{e}]]
Z_{e} = [[h_{ic} + (R_{1}∥R_{2}∥r_{s})|h_{fc}]]    Z_{o} = R_{E} ∥ Z_{e} ≈ Z_{e}

Resistances on the base side look (1+hfe) times smaller from the emitter. That is why Zo is only tens of ohms.

(c) Voltage gain.

v_{o} = h_{fc}i_{b}(R_{E}∥R_{L})    i_{b} = [[v_{i} − v_{o}|h_{ic}]]
v_{o}[1 + [[h_{fc}(R_{E}∥R_{L})|h_{ic}]]] = [[h_{fc}(R_{E}∥R_{L})|h_{ic}]] v_{i}
A_{v} = [[h_{fc}(R_{E}∥R_{L})|h_{ic} + h_{fc}(R_{E}∥R_{L})]] ≈ 1

Since hfc(RE∥RL) ≫ hic, the fraction is just under 1.

(d) Current gain and (e) power gain. Same method as CE (current divider at the input, then at the output):

A_{i} = [[h_{fc}R_{B}R_{E}|(R_{B}+Z_{b})(R_{E}+R_{L})]]    A_{p} = A_{v}A_{i} ≈ A_{i}

Power gain comes only from current gain, as the voltage gain is 1.

5.3 Worked example

R1 = 18k, R2 = 8.2k, RE = 2.7k, RL = 10k, rs = 600 Ω, hie = 1k, hfe = 100 (so hfc = 101).

  1. RB = 5.63k. R′ = RE∥RL = 2.7k∥10k = 2.13k.
  2. Zb = 1k + 101 × 2.13k = 215.7k. Zi = 5.63k ∥ 215.7k = 5.49 kΩ. Almost all of Zi comes from the bias resistors. Compare with the CE circuit: much higher Zb.
  3. Ze = (1k + 5.63k∥0.6k)/101 = (1000 + 542)/101 = 15.3 Ω. Zo = 2.7k ∥ 15.3 = 15.2 Ω.
  4. Av = 214.7k/(1k + 214.7k) = 0.995.
  5. Ai = 101 × 5.63k × 2.7k / (221.3k × 12.7k) = 0.547. It is below 1 because the bias network steals half the source current. Power gain Ap = 0.995 × 0.547 ≈ 0.54. (The transistor alone has ApT = Avhfc ≈ 100, the loss is in RB.)

Topic 6

Analysis of the CB amplifier

Likely asked as derivation or comparison

6.1 Circuit and intuition

Intuition

Input at the emitter, output at the collector, base shared (AC grounded). The input current is the emitter current, and almost all of it (α ≈ 0.98) comes out of the collector, so Ai ≈ α < 1. The input looks into the emitter: just re ≈ hib, a tiny resistance (tens of ohms). The output looks like a current source into RC, so Zo ≈ RC. A rising input at the emitter reduces vBE, so ic falls, which means no inversion: in phase.

The grounded base acts as a shield between emitter and collector, so there is no Miller multiplication, and the CB stage works at much higher frequencies than CE.

6.2 Derivations (neglect hrbVo, as in your notes)

Z_{e} ≈ h_{ib}    Z_{i} = Z_{e} ∥ R_{E} ≈ h_{ib} (R_{E} ≫ h_{ib})
Z_{c} ≈ 1/h_{ob} (very large)    Z_{o} = R_{C} ∥ Z_{c} ≈ R_{C}
v_{o} = i_{c}(R_{C}∥R_{L})    v_{i} = i_{e}h_{ib}
A_{v} = [[h_{fb}(R_{C}∥R_{L})|h_{ib}]] ≈ [[R_{C}∥R_{L}|r_{e}]]   (positive, magnitude of h_{fb} ≈ 0.98)
A_{i} = [[h_{fb}R_{E}R_{C}|(R_{E}+Z_{e})(R_{C}+R_{L})]]    A_{p} = A_{v}A_{i}

Transistor power gain: ApT = Av × hfb. Here RE shunts the input and divides the source current, just as RB did in CE.

6.3 Worked example

hib = 21.6 Ω, |hfb| = 0.98, hob = 0.49 µS, RE = 2.7k, RC = 2.2k, RL = 4.7k.

  • Zi = 2.7k ∥ 21.6 = 21.4 Ω
  • Zo = 2.2k ∥ (1/0.49 µS = 2.04 MΩ) = 2.20 kΩ
  • RC∥RL = 1.50k. Av = 0.98 × 1.50k/21.6 = +68
  • Ai = 0.98 × 2.7k × 2.2k/(2721.6 × 6900) = 0.31, Ap = 68 × 0.31 ≈ 21

Topic 8

Frequency response of BJT amplifiers

Asked: SDC-I Winter 2025 Q3 — “single stage CE amplifier and its typical frequency response”

8.1 What “frequency response” means

Intuition

Frequency response is the range of frequencies where the amplifier gives its desired voltage gain. An ideal amplifier would be flat from 0 Hz to infinity. A practical amplifier has a flat middle and rolls off at both ends, and that is fine, because we only need to cover the signal band (for audio, 20 Hz–20 kHz).

  • f1 = lower cut-off frequency. f2 = upper cut-off frequency.
  • At both the gain is 3 dB below the mid-band gain, which means Av/√2 (0.707 of mid gain).
  • 3 dB down also means half power. Check: mid gain 30 dB → Po/Pi = 103 = 1000. At 27 dB: 102.7 ≈ 501 ≈ 500. Half.
  • Bandwidth BW = f2 − f1.
Gain (dB) = 20 log [[v_{o}|v_{i}]] = 10 log [[P_{o}|P_{i}]]    BW = f_{2} − f_{1}

8.2 Why the gain falls at each end

Capacitors are the whole story. Their reactance is XC = 1/(2πfC): huge at low frequency, tiny at high frequency.

RegionCapacitor responsibleWhat happens
Low frequency (below f1)C1 input couplingForms a divider with Zi + rs. As f falls, XC1 rises and drops part of the signal.
C3 output couplingDivider with Zo + RL at the output.
C2 bypassXC2 grows, so RE is no longer shorted for AC and local negative feedback appears. Gain falls. Usually the dominant one.
High frequency (above f2)Cπ, Cμ inside the transistor, plus wiring and load capacitanceShunt the signal to ground. XC becomes small, so the collector signal is shorted and the base current is diverted.
C4 external shunt (if added)Deliberately sets the upper cut-off f2 (see the design topic).

Mid band: coupling and bypass capacitors act as shorts; internal capacitances act as open circuits. The gain is the plain Av = −hfe(RC∥RL)/hie.

8.3 The key relations

Bypass capacitor (lower cut-off). With RE bypassed only by C2:

A_{v} = −[[h_{fe}(R_{C}∥R_{L})|h_{ie} + (1+h_{fe})(R_{E}∥X_{C2})]] ≈ −[[h_{fe}(R_{C}∥R_{L})|h_{ie} − j(1+h_{fe})X_{C2}]] (R_{E} ≫ X_{C2})

The gain magnitude is mid-gain/√2 (−3 dB) when the two terms in the denominator are equal:

h_{ie} = (1+h_{fe}) X_{C2} at f_{1}  ⇒  C_{2} = [[1+h_{fe}|2π f_{1} h_{ie}]]

That is the smallest allowed C2. Choose the next larger standard value so f1 comes out slightly lower than needed.

Coupling capacitors. They should have very little effect on the response. XC1 and Zi form a potential divider, and XC3 and RL another. Make the capacitor reactance one-tenth of the resistance at f1:

X_{C1} = [[Z_{i} + r_{s}|10]]    X_{C3} = [[Z_{o} + R_{L}|10]]   at f_{1}

Shunt capacitor (upper cut-off). To get a chosen f2:

X_{C4} = R_{C} ∥ R_{L} at f_{2}  ⇒  C_{4} = [[1|2π f_{2} (R_{C}∥R_{L})]]

Inside the transistor (hybrid-π, extra). By the Miller effect the collector–base capacitance Cμ looks bigger at the input, multiplied by the gain:

C_{in} = C_{π} + C_{μ}(1 + g_{m}R′_{L})    f_{2} ≈ [[1|2π R_{th} C_{in}]]    f_{T} = [[g_{m}|2π(C_{π}+C_{μ})]]    f_{β} = [[f_{T}|β]]

Here fT is the frequency where the current gain falls to 1. More voltage gain means bigger Miller capacitance and lower f2: gain and bandwidth trade off. CB has no Miller effect, which is why it is fast.

Topic 9

Single-stage CE voltage amplifier design

Design problems follow one fixed recipe
Goal

Given the specification (gain, supply, load, source resistance, frequency range) find RC, RE, R1, R2, C1, C2, C3 and optionally C4. The circuit is the CE amplifier of Topic 4, with C2 bypass.

9.1 Design rules from your notes (and why)

  • Bigger gain needs bigger (RC∥RL), so pick RC as large as practical. But RC that is too large makes IC too small for the transistor.
  • IC ≥ 500 µA for most small-signal transistors. A good choice is 1 mA. (A higher IC also gives larger hfe on the data sheet, but then RC must be small.)
  • RC = VRC/IC: for a given IC the biggest RC needs the biggest drop VRC, so VCE and VE are kept small.
  • VCE ≥ 3 V to stay in the active region (this also allows about ±1 V swing).
  • No point in making RC larger than RL; ideally RC ≪ RL so RL hardly affects the gain.
  • VE ≫ VBE for good bias stability: normally VE ≈ 5 V, or 3 V if VCC < 10 V.
  • Bias divider current I2 = IC/10, so IB hardly disturbs VB.

Useful relations: VCC = VRC + VCE + VE. If hie is not on the data sheet: hie = hfeVT/IC, with VT = T/11600 (T in kelvin). Bias stability may also be given as S = (1+hfe)/[1 + hfe·RE/(RE+RB)], where RB = R1∥R2.

9.2 The 12 steps

  1. Select ICQ (1 mA).
  2. hie = hfeVT/IC.
  3. Av = Vo/Vs (magnitude).
  4. From Av = hfe(RC∥RL)/hie solve for RC. Algebra: Avhie(RC+RL) = hfeRCRL, so
    R_{C} = [[A_{v} h_{ie} R_{L}|h_{fe}R_{L} − A_{v}h_{ie}]]
  5. Share the rest of the supply equally: VCE = VE = (VCC − ICRC)/2. Check VCE ≥ 3 V.
  6. RE = VE/IE where IE = IC + IB = IC(1 + 1/hfe).
  7. I2 = IC/10. VB = VE + 0.7. R2 = VB/I2.
  8. R1 = (VCC − VB)/(I2 + IB).
  9. Bypass: C2 = (1+hfe)/(2π f1 hie).
  10. RB = R1∥R2, Zi = RB∥hie. Input coupling: C1 = 1/(2π f1(Zi+rs)/10).
  11. Zo = RC. Output coupling: C3 = 1/(2π f1(Zo+RL)/10).
  12. Shunt capacitor for the upper cut-off: C4 = 1/(2π f2(RC∥RL)).

Topic 10

Large-signal BJT model

Companion to Ebers-Moll (Topic 3) and the switch (Topic 11)
Intuition

The small-signal model only works for small wobbles. When the signal is big (a power amplifier or a switch) the operating point travels all the way across the characteristics. We then need a model valid over the whole range: the large-signal model. It describes DC currents and voltages, it is non-linear, and its parameters do not change with signal.

10.1 Regions of operation

RegionB-E junctionB-C junctionResultUse
CutoffReverse (or < 0.5 V)ReverseIB ≈ 0, IC ≈ 0, VCE ≈ VCCSwitch OFF

10.2 The model equations

Exponential (Ebers-Moll in active region):

I_{C} = I_{S} e^{V_{BE}/V_{T}} (1 + [[V_{CE}|V_{A}]])    I_{B} = [[I_{C}|β]]    I_{E} = (β+1) I_{B}

The (1 + VCE/VA) factor is the Early effect. Piecewise-linear models are the practical version, used by hand:

RegionModel
CutoffAll three terminals open. IB = IC = IE = 0.
ActiveVBE = 0.7 V (battery), IC = βIB (current source), ro optional.
SaturationVBE(sat) ≈ 0.8 V, VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V. Collector-emitter acts like a small battery.

10.3 How to find the region (method)

  1. Assume active. Find IB = (Vin − 0.7)/RB, then IC = βIB, then VCE = VCC − ICRC.
  2. If VCE > 0.2 V the assumption holds. Done.
  3. If VCE comes out below 0.2 V (or negative) the transistor is saturated. Redo with VCE = 0.2 V: IC(sat) = (VCC − 0.2)/RC.
  4. If Vin < 0.5 V, it is cutoff.

Example. VCC = 10 V, RC = 2 kΩ, β = 100, RB from VCC.

  • RB = 100 kΩ: IB = 93 µA, βIB = 9.3 mA would need VCE < 0. So saturated: IC = (10 − 0.2)/2k = 4.9 mA, VCE = 0.2 V. Here IC/IB = 53 < β.

Topic 11

BJT as a switch

Uses cutoff and saturation, the two ends of the load line
Intuition

A switch needs only two states. Slam the transistor to the two ends of the DC load line: cutoff (open switch, no current) and saturation (closed switch, as much current as the load allows). In between is wasted as heat, so we cross the active region as fast as we can.

OFF (cutoff)ON (saturation)
InputVin low (≈ 0 V, < 0.5 V)Vin high, with enough base current
IB0≥ IC(sat)/βmin
IC≈ 0 (only leakage ICEO)IC(sat) = (VCC − VCE(sat))/RC
VCE≈ VCC (switch open)VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V (switch closed)
Power in transistor≈ 0 (no current)≈ 0.2 V × IC (small)

11.1 Design recipe

  1. Load current:
    I_{C(sat)} = [[V_{CC} − V_{CE(sat)}|R_{C}]]
  2. Minimum base current: IB(sat) = IC(sat)/βmin. Use the lowest β on the data sheet, since β varies a lot.
  3. Overdrive for a firm “ON”: IB = ODF × IB(sat), ODF ≈ 2–10. The “forced β” is IC(sat)/IB = βmin/ODF.
  4. Base resistor:
    R_{B} = [[V_{in} − V_{BE(sat)}|I_{B}]]

Example. Drive a 12 V relay with 400 Ω coil from a 5 V logic signal, βmin = 100. IC(sat) = (12 − 0.2)/400 = 29.5 mA. IB(sat) = 0.295 mA. Take ODF = 5 → IB = 1.475 mA. RB = (5 − 0.8)/1.475 mA = 2.85 kΩ. Use the standard 2.7 kΩ (IB = 1.56 mA, ODF = 5.3). Add a flyback diode across the coil, because an inductive load produces a voltage spike when the current is cut.

11.2 Switching speed

  • td delay: base-emitter junction capacitances charge to ≈ 0.7 V before any collector current flows.
  • tr rise: IC goes from 10 % to 90 %.
  • ts storage: when the input goes low, the extra charge stored in the base (from over-driving) must first be removed. Usually the longest time. More overdrive means longer storage.
  • tf fall: IC goes from 90 % to 10 %.
  • Speed-up: moderate overdrive, a small speed-up capacitor across RB, or a Baker clamp (Schottky diode) to avoid deep saturation.

Applications: relay or LED driver, motor driver, logic inverter (input high gives output low, so it is a NOT gate), lamp or buzzer drive from a microcontroller pin.

If the transistor has the lowest β in its batch, the chosen IB must still be enough to saturate it. Using a typical β could leave a low-β unit in the active region, with high VCE and lots of heat.

Topic 12

Power BJT

Heat is the main design problem
Intuition

A small-signal BJT handles milliamps and milliwatts. A power BJT is built to carry amps and dissipate watts, in power amplifiers, regulators, motor drives and switching supplies. Everything about it is shaped by one fact: the heat produced at the collector junction must get out.

12.1 How it differs from a small-signal BJT

  • Large junction area and many parallel emitter “fingers” (interdigitated), to spread the current.
  • Thick, lightly doped collector region so it can block a high VCE (high VCEO).
  • Collector connected to the metal case or tab (TO-3, TO-220, TO-247) so heat flows into a heat sink. Because the case is the collector, it often needs an insulating washer.
  • Lower current gain (β falls at high current, typically 10–50), higher VCE(sat) (about 1 V), slower switching.
  • Key ratings: VCEO, VCBO, VEBO, IC(max), PD(max) (at 25 °C case), Tj(max) (150–200 °C).

12.2 Heat flow and heat sinks

Heat flows from the junction to the air through a chain of thermal resistances, exactly like current through resistors; power PD plays the role of current and temperature difference the role of voltage:

T_{j} = T_{a} + P_{D}(θ_{jc} + θ_{cs} + θ_{sa})    θ_{sa(max)} = [[T_{j(max)} − T_{a}|P_{D}]] − θ_{jc} − θ_{cs}

Derating: the allowed power falls as the case gets hotter: PD(TC) = PD(max) − k(TC − 25 °C), k in W/°C. Example: PD(max) = 100 W at 25 °C, k = 0.8 W/°C gives 60 W at TC = 75 °C and 0 W at 150 °C.

Example. Tj(max) = 150 °C, Ta = 40 °C, PD = 20 W, θjc = 1.5 °C/W, θcs = 0.5 °C/W. Total allowed θja = 110/20 = 5.5 °C/W, so the heat sink needs θsa ≤ 3.5 °C/W. A 3 °C/W sink would give Tj = 40 + 20 × 5 = 140 °C.

12.3 Thermal runaway and second breakdown

  • Thermal runaway: temperature rises → leakage ICBO roughly doubles every 10 °C and VBE falls about 2 mV/°C → IC rises → more heat → still hotter. A positive-feedback loop that can destroy the device. Cures: emitter resistor (negative feedback), good bias stabilisation, adequate heat sink, temperature-compensated bias.
  • Second breakdown: at high VCE and high IC the current crowds into hot spots, the local temperature explodes and the device fails below its PD limit. That is why the SOA has an extra, steeper boundary.

Darlington pair (two transistors, β ≈ β1β2) is often used in power stages when a very high current gain is needed from a weak driver. VBE becomes about 1.4 V.

Quick check: Why does the thermal runaway loop need a positive feedback?

Because more heat produces more collector current, and more collector current produces still more heat. Each step increases the next. An emitter resistor breaks the loop because a larger IC raises VE and reduces VBE.
