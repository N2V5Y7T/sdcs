Five ideas behind everything in this unit

If you finished Unit 1, 80% of this unit is “the BJT story again with a new control knob”. These five ideas show what is the same and what is different.

Idea 1 · A voltage-controlled valve

A BJT is a current-controlled valve: base current IB controls collector current. A FET is a voltage-controlled valve: the gate voltage VGS controls the drain current ID, and almost no current flows into the gate. Think of a garden hose with a clamp. You change the flow by squeezing (a voltage-like push on the clamp), and the clamp itself takes no water. That is why FET amplifiers have a huge input impedance.

Idea 2 · One word: transconductance

The BJT had β (current gain). The FET has gm (transconductance): how many mA of drain current you get per volt of gate voltage.

g_{m} = [[Δi_{D}|Δv_{GS}]]   (unit: siemens, mS = mA/V)

Every FET amplifier gain is just gm times a resistance. For a CS stage: Av = −gmRD′. If you remember only one formula in this unit, remember this one.

Idea 3 · Same four steps as Unit 1
  1. DC: find the Q-point (IDQ, VGSQ, VDSQ).
  2. Parameters at Q: compute gm (and rd or ro).
  3. AC equivalent: VDD → ground, capacitors → wires, transistor → small-signal model.
  4. Solve for Zi, Zo, Av.

Idea 5 · What is different from a BJT
  • The FET is a majority-carrier device (only electrons in an n-channel). Less noise, no minority-carrier storage, very good as a switch.
  • The current law is a square law: ID ∝ (VGS − Vx)². A BJT is exponential. So FET gm is smaller than a BJT’s at the same current, and so is the gain. Trade: much higher input impedance.
  • FET parameters vary a lot from one device to another (IDSS, VP, VT), so the Q-point must be set by circuit design, not by trusting data-sheet numbers.
Topic 1

JFET: structure, working and characteristics

Asked: Summer 2019 Q3 OR (characteristics, 10 marks) · Elements of Electronics (construction, 7 marks)Prerequisite for JFET models and amplifiers

1.1 What is a JFET and how is it built?

Intuition

Take a bar of n-type silicon. Current can flow along it from source (S) to drain (D) like water along a pipe. Now diffuse p-type regions on the two sides and join them: this is the gate (G). The p–n junction between each gate and the bar is kept reverse biased. A reverse-biased junction grows an insulating depletion region. The more negative VGS, the thicker that insulating layer, and the narrower the n-channel left for current. The gate is a pinch valve on the pipe, and it draws only the tiny reverse leakage current of a diode.

1.2 Why the drain curve flattens: pinch-off

Hold VGS = 0 and raise VDS from zero. Two things happen at once:

  1. Ohmic region (small VDS): the channel is wide, so ID rises almost linearly with VDS. The JFET behaves like a resistor whose value is set by VGS. This is the region used when the JFET works as a switch (Topic 6).
  2. Pinch-off: VDS also reverse-biases the gate junction, more at the drain end. The depletion layers thicken there and the channel narrows towards the drain. At VDS = |VP| (for VGS = 0) the channel nearly closes at the drain end.
  3. Active (saturation) region: beyond pinch-off, the extra VDS is dropped across the short pinched section. The current is fixed by how narrow the channel is, which only VGS controls. So ID stays nearly constant when VDS rises. This flat region is the amplifier region: the JFET acts as a gate-controlled current source.
  4. Breakdown: at a large VDS the gate–drain junction avalanches and ID shoots up. Never operate there.

Key points on the curves. IDSS is the drain current with VGS = 0 in the active region (the maximum ID). VP = VGS(off) is the gate voltage that turns the channel completely off (negative for n-channel, positive for p-channel). The pinch-off locus VDS = VGS − VP separates ohmic from active.

n-channel vs p-channel

A p-channel JFET is the mirror image: all voltages and currents reverse sign. VGS is positive, VP is positive, VDD is negative, and ID flows from source to drain. The curves look the same when drawn with magnitudes. Say this in one line; the exam question on “N-channel and P-channel” wants both sketches.

1.3 The Shockley equation

I_{D} = I_{DSS}(1 − [[V_{GS}|V_{GS(off)}]])^{2}    for V_{GS(off)} ≤ V_{GS} ≤ 0
Inverse form: V_{GS} = V_{GS(off)}(1 − √[[I_{D}|I_{DSS}]])   (use this when you choose a Q-point)

The transfer curve is a half parabola. Check three points to sketch it fast: VGS = 0 gives IDSS; VGS = VP/2 gives IDSS/4; VGS = VP gives 0.

1.4 Biasing and the Q-point

An amplifier needs a Q-point in the active region with room to swing. With voltage-divider bias (the circuit your notes use), the gate is held at VG and the source resistor creates VS:

V_{G} = V_{DD}[[R_{2}|R_{1}+R_{2}]]    V_{S} = I_{D}R_{S}    V_{GS} = V_{G} − I_{D}R_{S}    V_{DS} = V_{DD} − I_{D}(R_{D}+R_{S})

Combine the bias line (VGS = VG − IDRS) with the Shockley curve. Their intersection is the Q-point. A bigger RS gives a flatter bias line and a more stable Q-point (negative feedback), exactly like RE in a BJT.

Why the gate voltage can be negative-looking

For an n-channel JFET we need VGS ≤ 0. In the divider circuit VG is positive, but VS = IDRS is more positive, so VGS = VG − VS comes out negative. That is the trick: the source is lifted above the gate.

JFET parameters and small-signal model

Asked: Summer 2019 Q3 OR — “define the terms gm, rd and μ”

2.1 The three parameters

gm transconductanceΔiD/ΔvGS at constant VDSSlope of the transfer curve. Control strength of the gate. Unit: S (mS = mA/V).

Why μ = gmrd. A change ΔvGS changes the current by gmΔvGS. To bring the current back we must change vDS so that ΔvDS/rd cancels it: ΔvDS = −gmrdΔvGS. Hence |ΔvDS/ΔvGS| = gmrd.

2.2 Getting gm from the Shockley equation

Differentiate ID = IDSS(1 − VGS/VP)² with respect to VGS:

g_{m} = [[dI_{D}|dV_{GS}]] = [[2I_{DSS}|−V_{P}]](1 − [[V_{GS}|V_{P}]]) = g_{m0}(1 − [[V_{GS}|V_{P}]])
g_{m0} = [[2I_{DSS}||V_{GS(off)}|]]    g_{m} = g_{m0}√[[I_{D}|I_{DSS}]]

So gm is largest at VGS = 0 and shrinks to zero at pinch-off. A JFET biased at a smaller current has less gain.

Worked check (from your notes)

IDSS = 20 mA, VGS(off) = −5 V. Find ID and gm at VGS = −4 V.

gm0 = 2(20 mA)/5 V = 8 mS. ID = 20(1 − 4/5)² = 20(0.2)² = 0.8 mA. gm = 8(1 − 4/5) = 1.6 mS. Check: 8√(0.8/20) = 8(0.2) = 1.6 mS ✓. Near pinch-off the JFET gives only a fifth of its maximum gm.

2.3 Why a voltage change becomes a voltage gain (physical picture)

Your notes use this example: ID = 2 mA, VDD = 20 V, RD = 6 kΩ, gm = 2 mS.

  1. No signal: VDS = 20 − 2 mA × 6 kΩ = 8 V.
  2. Add +50 mV at the gate: ΔID = gmΔVGS = 2 mS × 50 mV = 0.1 mA.
  3. That extra current drops more voltage in RD: ΔVo = −0.1 mA × 6 kΩ = −0.6 V. Drain falls from 8 V to 7.4 V.
  4. −50 mV at the gate: drain rises to 8.6 V. So Av = −0.6/0.05 = −12. The minus sign means the output is inverted.
A_{v} = −g_{m}R_{D} (= −2 mS × 6 kΩ = −12)

Topic 3

Analysis of JFET CS, CD and CG amplifiers

Derive Zi, Zo, Av · 10 marks eachSame method as Unit 1: AC equivalent, then solve

3.0 The common recipe

  1. Draw the circuit with voltage-divider bias. RG = R1∥R2 is what the source sees at the gate.
  2. VDD → AC ground. Capacitors → short circuits.
  3. Replace the JFET by the model: RGS, gmvgs, rd.
  4. Find Zi (looking into the input), Zo (looking into the output with the source off and RL removed), Av = vo/vi.

3.1 Common-source (CS)

Intuition

Input at the gate, output at the drain, source is the common terminal. It is the FET version of the CE amplifier: big voltage gain, inversion, very high input impedance, medium output impedance. It is the “main” FET voltage amplifier.

(a) RS bypassed. The source is at AC ground.

Z_{g} = R_{GS}  ⇒  Z_{i} = R_{G}∥R_{GS} ≈ R_{G}
Z_{d} = r_{d}  ⇒  Z_{o} = R_{D}∥r_{d}  (R_{L} removed)
v_{o} = i_{d}(r_{d}∥R_{D}∥R_{L}), i_{d} = −g_{m}v_{i}  ⇒  A_{v} = −g_{m}(r_{d}∥R_{D}∥R_{L}) ≈ −g_{m}(R_{D}∥R_{L})

(b) RS not bypassed. Now the drain current flows through RS, so the source voltage rises and cancels part of the input. This is negative feedback (source degeneration).

v_{i} = v_{gs} + i_{d}R_{S} = v_{gs}(1 + g_{m}R_{S})  ⇒  v_{gs} = [[v_{i}|1 + g_{m}R_{S}]]
Z_{g} = [[v_{i}|i_{g}]] = R_{GS}(1 + g_{m}R_{S})  ⇒  Z_{i} = R_{G}∥Z_{g}
Z_{d} = r_{d}(1 + g_{m}R_{S}) + R_{S}  ⇒  Z_{o} = R_{D}∥Z_{d}
A_{v} ≈ −[[g_{m}(R_{D}∥R_{L})|1 + g_{m}R_{S}]] = −[[R_{D}∥R_{L}|R_{S} + 1/g_{m}]] ≈ −[[R_{D}∥R_{L}|R_{S}]] (if g_{m}R_{S} ≫ 1)
What the degeneration buys

Gain drops by the factor (1 + gmRS), but the gain becomes almost independent of gm, which varies a lot between devices. It also stabilises the bias, reduces distortion and raises Zo and the bandwidth. “Lose gain, gain stability” is the one-line summary.

Worked example 1 (notes): CS, RS not bypassed

gm = 2.5 mS, rd = 50 kΩ, RD = 6 kΩ, RS = 1 kΩ, RL = 12 kΩ, RG = 1 MΩ.

  1. Zi ≈ RG = 1 MΩ (RGS is far larger).
  2. Zd = rd + RS + gmRSrd = 50 + 1 + (2.5)(1)(50) = 176 kΩ. Zo = 6k ∥ 176k = 5.80 kΩ.
  3. RD∥RL = 6k ∥ 12k = 4 kΩ.
  4. Av = −(2.5)(4)/(1 + 2.5×1) = −10/3.5 = −2.86.

3.2 Common-drain (CD), the source follower

Intuition

Input at the gate, output at the source, drain at AC ground (the supply). The output copies the input, only about 0.7 to 0.99 of it. If the gate rises 1 V, the source rises almost 1 V and VGS barely changes. The device acts as a buffer: it presents a huge impedance to the source and a small one to the load.

Let RS′ = rd∥RS∥RL. The controlled current gmvgs flows into the source node through RS′:

v_{o} = g_{m}v_{gs}R_{S}′    v_{i} = v_{gs} + v_{o} = v_{gs}(1 + g_{m}R_{S}′)
A_{v} = [[g_{m}R_{S}′|1 + g_{m}R_{S}′]]  (0 < A_{v} < 1, in phase)    Z_{i} = R_{G}∥Z_{g} ≈ R_{G}, Z_{g} ≈ R_{GS}(1 + g_{m}R_{S}′)

Output impedance. Set vi = 0 and look into the source. Any rise in the source voltage reduces vgs, so the device “fights back” like a resistor 1/gm:

Z_{s} ≈ [[1|g_{m}]]    Z_{o} = R_{S}∥Z_{s} ≈ R_{S}∥[[1|g_{m}]]
Worked example 2 (notes): CD

gm = 4 mS, rd = 40 kΩ, RS = 2.2 kΩ, RL = 4.7 kΩ, RG = 1 MΩ.

  1. Zi ≈ 1 MΩ.
  2. Zs ≈ 1/gm = 250 Ω. Zo = 2.2k ∥ 250 Ω = 224.5 Ω.
  3. RS′ = 40k ∥ 2.2k ∥ 4.7k = 1.444 kΩ.
  4. Av = (4)(1.444)/(1 + 4×1.444) = 5.78/6.78 = +0.85.

3.3 Common-gate (CG)

Intuition

Input at the source, output at the drain, gate at AC ground through a bypass capacitor. The signal pushes straight into the source, where the resistance is only about 1/gm, so Zi is low. When the source goes up, VGS goes down, the drain current falls, and the drain voltage rises. So there is no inversion. Used where a low input impedance or a wide bandwidth is needed (like the CB stage).

Z_{i} = R_{S}∥[[1|g_{m}]] ≈ [[1|g_{m}]]    Z_{o} = R_{D}∥r_{d}    A_{v} = g_{m}(r_{d}∥R_{D}∥R_{L}) ≈ g_{m}(R_{D}∥R_{L})

If the gate bypass capacitor is missing the gate follows the source through RG and RGS, so vgs = −viRGS/(RG+RGS) and the gain falls by that same fraction; Zi ≈ (1/gm)(RG+RGS)/RGS.

Worked example 3 (notes): CG

gm = 5 mS, rd = 60 kΩ, RS = 1 kΩ, RD = 4.7 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ.

  1. Zs = 1/gm = 200 Ω. Zi = 1k ∥ 200 = 166.7 Ω.
  2. Zo = 4.7k ∥ 60k = 4.36 kΩ.
  3. RD(ac) = 60k ∥ 4.7k ∥ 10k = 3.036 kΩ. Av = 5 mS × 3.036 kΩ = +15.18.

Topic 4

Frequency response of JFET amplifiers

Not in your Unit 2 notes: standard theory, same ideas as BJT Topic 8

4.1 Why the gain falls at both ends

Intuition

In the mid-band, capacitors look like wires or like open circuits, and the answer is a flat gain Av = −gmRL′. Two kinds of capacitor spoil that:

  • Big external capacitors (C1, CS, C3) are wires only if the frequency is high enough. At low frequency they pass less signal, so the gain falls. That sets the lower cut-off f1.
  • Tiny internal capacitances (Cgs, Cgd, Cds, plus wiring) are open circuits at low frequency but leak signal away at high frequency. That sets the upper cut-off f2.

The band between f1 and f2, where the gain stays within 3 dB (0.707×) of the mid-band value, is the bandwidth.

4.2 High-frequency model

The Miller effect in one paragraph

Cgd connects the input (gate) to the output (drain), and the drain moves in opposite direction by A times as much. So when the gate rises by 1 V, the voltage across Cgd changes by (1 + A) V, and the capacitor must supply (1 + A) times the charge. From the input it looks like a capacitor (1 + A) times bigger.

C_{i} = C_{gs} + C_{gd}(1 + g_{m}R_{L}′)    C_{o} = C_{ds} + C_{gd}(1 + [[1|g_{m}R_{L}′]]) ≈ C_{ds} + C_{gd}
f_{in} = [[1|2π R_{sig} C_{i}]]    f_{out} = [[1|2π R_{L}′ C_{o}]]    f_{2} ≈ [[1|√(1/f_{in}² + 1/f_{out}²)]]

Rsig is the resistance the gate sees: rs∥RG (small, so the input pole is usually the first to bite). Higher gain → larger Miller capacitance → lower f2.

Unity-gain frequency: fT = gm / [2π(Cgs + Cgd)]. It tells you where the intrinsic device stops amplifying.

4.3 Low-frequency poles

The overall f1 is a little larger than the biggest of the three. A fast estimate is √(fC1² + fCS² + fC3²). The source bypass capacitor is normally the culprit because it sees a low resistance (about 1/gm). That is why design rules give CS the biggest value.

4.4 Worked example

gm = 3 mS, RL′ = 4 kΩ, source seen at gate 50 kΩ, Cgs = 5 pF, Cgd = 2 pF, Cds = 1 pF. C1 = 0.01 µF (sees 1 MΩ), CS = 10 µF (RS = 1 kΩ), C3 = 0.1 µF (sees 20 kΩ).

  1. Mid-band: A = 3 × 4 = 12 (21.6 dB).
  2. Ci = 5 + 2(1 + 12) = 31 pF. fin = 1/(2π × 50 k × 31 p) = 102.7 kHz.
  3. Co = 1 + 2(1 + 1/12) ≈ 3.2 pF. fout = 1/(2π × 4 k × 3.2 p) ≈ 12.6 MHz. So f2 ≈ 102.7 kHz, set by the input Miller pole.
  4. fC1 = 15.9 Hz, fCS = (1+3×1)/(2π × 1 k × 10 µ) = 63.7 Hz, fC3 = 79.6 Hz. f1 ≈ 103 Hz.
  5. Bandwidth ≈ 102.6 kHz. fT = 3 mS/[2π × 7 pF] ≈ 68 MHz.

Topic 5

Single-stage JFET CS amplifier design

Asked: Summer 2019 Q3 (design of a gate bias circuit, 10 marks)Method from your notes (Section 10)

5.1 The idea

You are given a gain, a supply and a load. You choose a Q-point and then find RD, RS, R1, R2. Three steps, in your notes’ order:

  1. Select the Q-point. Pick IDQ (below IDSS) and VDSQ. Then VGSQ = VP(1 − √(IDQ/IDSS)) and gm = gm0√(IDQ/IDSS).
  2. Find RD and RS. The DC loop gives RD + RS = K1 = (VDD − VDSQ)/IDQ. The gain with RS unbypassed is Av = (RD∥RL)/(RS + 1/gm). Put RS = K1 − RD, let K = K1 + 1/gm and A = |Av|:
A R_{D}² − [A(K − R_{L}) − R_{L}] R_{D} − A K R_{L} = 0

Solve the quadratic. Keep the root with 0 < RD < K1, then RS = K1 − RD. If no root fits, that gain is not possible with that Q-point.

  1. Gate divider. VG = VGSQ + IDQRS. With the required RG = R1∥R2:
R_{1} = R_{G}[[V_{DD}|V_{G}]]    R_{2} = R_{G}[[V_{DD}|V_{DD} − V_{G}]]

Finally round to standard values, recompute the DC point and check: active region, gain, and swing.

Your notes’ formulas for R1, R2 were corrected

The notes explicitly say the gate-divider formulas were corrected, so use the ones above. They follow from VG = VDDR2/(R1+R2) and RG = R1R2/(R1+R2). Divide the second by the first and you get R1 = RGVDD/VG.

5.2 Worked example (made to follow your method)

IDSS = 10 mA, VP = −4 V, VDD = 24 V, IDQ = 4 mA, VDSQ = 8 V, |Av| = 3, RL = 10 kΩ, RG = 1 MΩ.

  1. VGSQ = −4(1 − √0.4) = −1.47 V. gm0 = 2(10)/4 = 5 mS, gm = 5√0.4 = 3.16 mS.
  2. K1 = (24 − 8)/4 = 4 kΩ. K = 4 + 1/3.16 = 4.316 kΩ.
  3. Quadratic: 3RD² − [3(4.316 − 10) − 10]RD − 3(4.316)(10) = 0 → 3RD² + 27.05RD − 129.5 = 0 → RD = 3.46 kΩ.
  4. RS = 4 − 3.46 = 0.54 kΩ. Check gain: (3.46∥10)/(0.54 + 0.316) = 2.57/0.857 = 3.0 ✓.
  5. VG = −1.47 + 4 mA × 0.54 k = 0.69 V. R1 = 1 M × 24/0.69 = 34.7 MΩ, R2 = 1 M × 24/(24 − 0.69) = 1.03 MΩ.
Watch the sign of VG

If VG comes out negative, a divider to ground cannot make it. That means RS is too small, so the gain you asked for is too high for this Q-point. Reduce the gain or use self-bias. The widget below shows this warning.

6.1 Idea: a resistor you can switch

Intuition

In the ohmic region (small VDS) the JFET channel is just a resistor rds, and its value is set by the gate voltage. With VGS = 0 the channel is widest and rds is smallest, ON (typically 50 to 500 Ω). With VGS at or below VP the channel is pinched off and rds is enormous (10⁹ Ω or more), OFF. So the gate voltage is a control knob on a resistor, and the signal passes through the channel.

Topic 7

MOSFET: structure, regions and characteristics

Asked: Summer 2019 Q4 OR — “difference between D-MOSFET and E-MOSFET with constructional diagram” (10 marks)

7.1 Enhancement MOSFET (E-MOSFET)

Intuition

Start with a p-type slab (the body). Put two n⁺ islands in it: source and drain. Between them there is no n-type path, just p-type material, so the two junctions back-to-back block current. Now put a thin oxide (SiO2) over the gap and a metal gate on it. Apply a positive gate voltage. The gate and the body form a capacitor, and the positive gate pulls electrons up to the surface and pushes holes away. Above a certain voltage, the threshold VT, the surface has more electrons than holes. It has been inverted into n-type: an induced channel joins source and drain. The more VGS exceeds VT, the more electrons, the lower the channel resistance. “Enhancement” means the channel is enhanced by the gate; at VGS = 0 the device is OFF.

Why the gate current is zero. The oxide is an insulator. The input is a capacitor, so IG ≈ 0 at DC and the DC input resistance is 10¹² Ω or more.

7.2 Depletion MOSFET (D-MOSFET): not in your notes

The D-MOSFET has a built-in n-channel, diffused under the oxide during manufacture. Current flows at VGS = 0 (IDSS). A negative VGS repels electrons and depletes the channel until it closes at VP. A positive VGS attracts more electrons, enhancing the channel beyond IDSS. So it works in both depletion and enhancement mode, and it follows the same Shockley equation as the JFET.

12.1 The switch

Intuition

A good switch is either a very high resistance (OFF) or a very low resistance (ON). The E-MOSFET does exactly this: VGS ≤ VT → no channel → open. VGS far above VT with small VDS → triode → a resistor RDS(on) = 1/(KnVOV) of a few milliohms to ohms. Saturation is the wrong state for an ON switch: VDS is large and the transistor wastes power. Amplifiers live in saturation; switches live in cutoff and deep triode.

12.2 Resistor-load NOT gates and their problem

Topic 13

Power MOSFET

13.1 Why it is built differently

Intuition

A small-signal MOSFET has source and drain side by side on the chip surface, so current runs sideways in a thin film: great for logic, but the current and voltage are limited. A power MOSFET puts the drain on the back of the chip, so current flows vertically through the whole chip thickness. Thousands of cells in parallel give a large current (big area → low RDS(on)). A lightly doped n⁻ drift layer sits between the body and the drain; it supports the blocking voltage: thicker layer = higher VDSS, but also higher RDS(on). That is the main trade-off.

The inherent p-body/n⁻ junction forms a body diode from source to drain. It conducts when the drain goes below the source (useful as a freewheel path, but with reverse-recovery loss).

13.3 Gate charge and the Miller plateau

The gate is a capacitor, so every switching event needs charge. The turn-on sequence:

  1. Delay: the gate driver charges Cgs until VGS reaches Vth. Nothing happens to ID yet.
  2. Current rise: ID rises to the load current while VDS is still high (power is large: V × I overlap).
  3. Miller plateau: VGS stays almost constant while the driver charges Cgd (charge Qgd). The drain voltage falls rapidly here.
  4. Full enhancement: VGS rises to the drive level; RDS(on) reaches its final low value.

Design trade-off in one line

A lower RDS(on) means a bigger die and a bigger Qg: you gain conduction loss but lose switching speed and drive power. Choose by comparing both loss terms at your frequency.

Quick check: why must you not drive a power MOSFET only to VGS(th)?

VGS(th) is only where a tiny test current begins to flow. RDS(on) is specified at a much higher gate voltage (often 10 V). Below that the channel is poor and the device dissipates too much power.
