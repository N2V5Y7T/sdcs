Start here

Six ideas behind everything in this unit

A single transistor stage rarely gives enough gain or the right input and output resistance. Unit 3 is about joining stages and understanding what the joining does.

Idea 1 · Stages load each other

The input resistance of stage 2 is a real resistor hanging on the collector of stage 1. So stage 1’s gain must be calculated with stage 2 connected. Multiplying the unloaded gains of the two stages is the classic wrong answer, and it gives a result that is far too big.

Idea 2 · Gain of each stage = −gm × (its own loaded collector resistance)
A_{v1} = −g_{m1}R_{C1}′,   R_{C1}′ = R_{C1} ∥ r_{o1} ∥ R_{i2}  →  A_{v} = A_{v1}A_{v2}

The “next stage’s Ri” is the only new idea compared with Unit 1. The rest is hybrid-π from Unit 1.

Idea 3 · Ri comes from the first stage, Ro from the last

The source only ever sees the first transistor’s input. The load only ever sees the last transistor’s output. Everything in between changes gain, not what the outside world sees (to first order). That is why a follower is put first when you need high Ri and last when you need low Ro.

Idea 4 · Every capacitor creates a corner frequency

Coupling and bypass capacitors kill the gain at low frequency. Junction capacitances (and the Miller multiplication of Cμ) kill the gain at high frequency. Between the corners is the mid-band, where all the “gain = −gmR′” formulas are valid.

Idea 5 · Cascading narrows the bandwidth

Each added stage adds another pole at each end. The −3 dB point of the combination is reached sooner: fL goes up, fH goes down. More gain costs bandwidth.

Idea 6 · Three coupling methods and two special stages
  • RC coupling: a capacitor between stages. Independent bias, no DC passes.
  • Direct coupling: a wire between stages. DC passes, so bias and drift are shared.
  • Transformer coupling: a transformer. Matches impedances, delivers power.
  • Darlington: two transistors as one, huge current gain. Cascode: CE on CB, high gain with wide bandwidth.
Topic 1

BJT behaviour at low, mid and high frequency

Base of every RC-coupled question, including “derive the mid-band gain”Notes: Section 1–2

1.1 The three regions

Intuition

A capacitor’s reactance is 1/(2πfC). At low frequency this is large, so a series coupling capacitor blocks the signal and a bypass capacitor stops bypassing. At high frequency the reactance is small, so tiny capacitances inside the transistor, which are open circuits at audio, start to short the signal away. In between, the big capacitors are shorts and the tiny ones are opens: a clean region with constant gain.

RegionCoupling / bypass CJunction C (Cπ, Cμ)Model to use
Low (f < fL)Reactance comparable to resistors: they set the cornersOpenMid-band model + the external capacitors
MidShort circuitsOpen circuitsPlain hybrid-π, gain constant
High (f > fH)ShortReactance comparable to resistors: they set the cornersHigh-frequency hybrid-π with Cπ, Cμ

1.2 Mid-band parameters (revision of Unit 1)

g_{m} = [[I_{C}|V_{T}]],   r_{π} = [[β|g_{m}]],   r_{e} ≈ [[1|g_{m}]],   r_{o} ≈ [[V_{A}|I_{C}]]   (V_{T} ≈ 25 mV)
A_{v} ≈ −g_{m}R_{C}′  (emitter bypassed)    A_{v} ≈ −[[g_{m}R_{C}′|1 + g_{m}R_{E}]]  (emitter not bypassed)

where RC′ = RC ∥ ro ∥ RL (or ∥ Ri of the next stage in a cascade).

Worked Problem 1 (notes): mid-band CE parameters and gain

IC = 1.5 mA, β = 120, VA = 90 V, RC = 3.3 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ, emitter bypassed.

gm = 1.5 mA/25 mV = 60 mS. rπ = 120/0.06 = 2.0 kΩ. re = 1/gm = 16.7 Ω. ro = 90/1.5 mA = 60 kΩ.

RC′ = 3.3k ∥ 10k ∥ 60k = 2.383 kΩ, so Av ≈ −(0.06)(2383) = −143.

1.3 Low-frequency response: three capacitors, three corners

Look at the single CE stage with input coupling C1, emitter bypass CE and output coupling C2. For each capacitor, ask one question: what total resistance does this capacitor see, looking out of its two terminals with all sources turned off? The corner frequency is then 1/(2π × that resistance × C).

  1. C1 (input). Sees the source resistance in series with the amplifier’s input resistance: Ri = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ rπ. So fp1 = 1/[2πC1(rs + Ri)].
  2. C2 (output). Sees the output resistance Ro = RC ∥ ro in series with RL. So fp2 = 1/[2πC2(Ro + RL)].
  3. CE (bypass). Sees RE in parallel with the small resistance looking into the emitter, (rπ + RB)/(β + 1), where RB = rs ∥ R1 ∥ R2. That is only a few tens of ohms, so CE must be hundreds of µF. This is why bypass capacitors are the big electrolytics.
C_{1} ≥ [[1|2πf_{p1}(r_{s}+R_{i})]],   C_{2} ≥ [[1|2πf_{p2}(R_{o}+R_{L})]],   C_{E} ≥ [[1|2πf_{pE}R_{E,seen}]],   R_{E,seen} = R_{E} ∥ [[r_{π}+R_{B}|β+1]]

With several corners, the highest one dominates fL. In a design you place each individual pole below the required fL (or make one intentionally dominant) because the corners add up in the overall response.

Worked Problem 2 (notes): design C1, CE, C2 for 20 Hz poles

rs = 600 Ω, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, rπ = 2.5 kΩ, β = 100, RE = 1 kΩ, RC = 4.7 kΩ, ro = 100 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ.

Ri = 47k ∥ 10k ∥ 2.5k = 1.918 kΩ → C1 = 1/[2π(20)(600 + 1918)] = 3.16 µF.

RB = 600 ∥ 47k ∥ 10k = 559 Ω → RE,seen = 1k ∥ (2500+559)/101 = 29.4 Ω → CE = 1/[2π(20)(29.4)] = 271 µF.

Ro = 4.7k ∥ 100k = 4.489 kΩ → C2 = 1/[2π(20)(4489 + 10000)] = 0.549 µF.

Standard values: 4.7 µF, 330 µF, 0.68 µF (round up, which moves the pole lower).

Quick check 1: why is CE so much larger than C1?

CE sees only ~30 Ω (the emitter-side resistance), while C1 sees kΩ. For the same corner frequency C ∝ 1/R, so CE must be about 100 times larger.

Quick check 2: the mid-band gain is 40 dB. At what gain are fL and fH?

At 37 dB (3 dB below mid-band), i.e. at 0.707 of the mid-band voltage gain.

Topic 2

High-frequency response, Miller effect and fT

Cascode (Topic 10) is only understandable after this topicNotes: Section 2.3; Worked Problem 3

2.1 The high-frequency hybrid-π model

Intuition

A transistor has two p–n junctions. Each junction stores charge and so behaves like a capacitor. The base–emitter junction (forward biased) gives Cπ (also written Ce), tens of pF. The base–collector junction (reverse biased) gives Cμ (Cc), a few pF. Add the base spreading resistance rbb′ (the resistance of the thin base between the pin and the active region) and the model is complete.

2.2 Why Cμ is the troublemaker: Miller theorem

Cμ sits between the input node (B′) and the output node (C), where the voltage is a large, inverted copy of the input. Miller’s theorem lets you replace it by two grounded capacitors, one at each end.

Derivation (added; the notes give the result)

Current leaving node 1 through Z: I = (v1 − v2)/Z = v1(1 − K)/Z. So the impedance seen at the input is v1/I:

Z_{1} = [[Z|1 − K]]

Current entering node 2 through Z: I = (v2 − v1)/Z with v1 = v2/K, so I = v2(K − 1)/(KZ). Seen from the output:

Z_{2} = [[ZK|K − 1]]

For a capacitor Z = 1/(jωCμ), and with K = Avbc a large negative number:

C_{Mi} = C_{μ}(1 − A_{vbc}) = C_{μ}(1 + |A_{vbc}|)    C_{Mo} = C_{μ}(1 − [[1|A_{vbc}]]) ≈ C_{μ}

Physical meaning. When the base rises by 1 mV, the collector falls by |Av| mV. Across Cμ the voltage changes by (1 + |Av|) mV, so it draws (1 + |Av|) times the charging current. From the input it looks like a capacitor (1 + |Av|) times bigger. A 4 pF capacitor with gain −40 looks like 164 pF.

2.3 Input and output poles

C_{in} = C_{π} + C_{μ}(1 − A_{vbc}),   f_{Hi} = [[1|2πR_{Hi}C_{in}]]    f_{Ho} = [[1|2πR_{Ho}C_{Mo}]]

RHi is the resistance across Cin: rπ in parallel with (rbb′ + rs ∥ R1 ∥ R2). RHo is the resistance at the collector node, RC ∥ ro ∥ RL. The lower of the two poles dominates fH. In almost every high-gain CE stage it is the input pole, because of the Miller multiplication.

Worked Problem 3 (notes): Miller effect and upper cut-off

Cπ = 25 pF, Cμ = 4 pF, Avbc = −40, RHi = 5 kΩ, RHo = 3 kΩ.

Cin = 25 + 4[1 − (−40)] = 189 pF. fHi = 1/[2π(5k)(189p)] = 168 kHz.

CMo = 4(1 − 1/(−40)) = 4.10 pF. fHo = 1/[2π(3k)(4.10p)] ≈ 12.9 MHz.

The input pole is 77 times lower, so the Miller-multiplied input capacitance sets fH.

2.4 The transition frequency fT

fT is the frequency at which the short-circuit current gain |hfe| falls to 1. With the output shorted, the current gain is β/(1 + jωrπ(Cπ + Cμ)), which falls to 1 at

f_{T} = [[g_{m}|2π(C_{π} + C_{μ})]]

It measures how fast the transistor itself is. For gm = 40 mS, Cπ = 25 pF, Cμ = 4 pF: fT ≈ 220 MHz. (The expression above is the notes’ formula; the one-line derivation is added.) A usable amplifier bandwidth is always far below fT because of Miller multiplication.

Exam traps
  • Miller multiplies Cμ by (1 + |Av|) only at the input. At the output it is about Cμ.
  • Avbc is the gain from the internal base B′ to the collector, not from the source. Use −gmRC′.
  • Do not mix the notation: Ce = Cπ and Cc = Cμ. The notes use both.
10-mark answer skeleton: “Explain the high-frequency response of a CE amplifier”
  1. High-frequency hybrid-π figure with rbb′, rπ, Cπ, Cμ, gmvb′e, rce (3).
  2. Statement and derivation of Miller’s theorem (3).
  3. Cin = Cπ + Cμ(1 + gmRC′), CMo ≈ Cμ and the two poles (2).
  4. fH set by the lower pole; fT formula (2).
Quick check 1: gain −100, Cμ = 3 pF. Miller input capacitance?

3 × (1 + 100) = 303 pF.

Quick check 2: why does halving the gain roughly double fH in a high-gain stage?

When |Av| ≫ 1 the Miller term dominates Cin, so Cin ∝ |Av| and fHi ∝ 1/|Av|. That is the gain–bandwidth trade-off (a standard result, not in the notes).

Topic 3

Why multistage? Classification and coupling methods

Notes: Section 3 · Short answer, 4–6 marks

3.1 The need

Intuition

One CE stage gives a gain of perhaps 50 to 150. A microphone gives millivolts and a speaker needs volts. You need 1000 or more. Also, a single stage has one input resistance and one output resistance, and they are rarely what you need: a high-resistance sensor wants a high Ri (use a follower first), and a low-resistance speaker wants a low Ro (use a follower or power stage last). So you chain stages, each doing one job.

  • Gain: the total is the product of the stage gains (or the sum, in dB).
  • Impedance matching: choose each stage’s Ri and Ro for the neighbours.
  • Bandwidth and drive: a wideband stage, a power stage and so on.

3.2 Classification

Multistage amplifiers are classified in three ways. In an exam, name all three.

  • By coupling: RC, direct, transformer.
  • By signal type: small-signal voltage amplifier stages or large-signal (power) output stages.
  • By configuration of the stages: CE–CE, CE–CC, CC–CE, CE–CB (cascode), Darlington.

3.3 The three coupling methods

MethodMain advantageMain limitationTypical use
RC couplingSimple, cheap, independent DC bias of each stageLow-frequency roll-off; interstage loading; cannot amplify DCGeneral voltage amplification, audio
Direct couplingNo coupling capacitor; response extends to DCDC bias and drift of each stage affect the nextDC and very-low-frequency amplifiers, ICs
Transformer couplingImpedance matching, DC isolation, power transferBulky, expensive; bandwidth limited by transformer non-idealitiesPower/output stages

Topic 4

General parameter evaluation: loading, Ri, Ro, Av, Ai, bandwidth

The method behind every numerical in this unitNotes: Section 4; Worked Problems 4 and 5

4.1 Interstage loading

  1. Work from the load backwards for the gains. Stage 2 has the real load RL, so find Av2 first (RC2′ = RC2 ∥ ro2 ∥ RL).
  2. Find Ri2. It is the load that stage 1 sees.
  3. Then stage 1: RC1′ = RC1 ∥ ro1 ∥ Ri2 and Av1 = −gm1RC1′.
  4. Multiply: Av = Av1Av2. The sign gives the phase: two inverting stages give a positive (in-phase) result.
A_{v} = A_{v1}A_{v2}…,   A_{vs} = A_{v}[[R_{i}|R_{s}+R_{i}]],   A_{i} = [[i_{o}|i_{i}]] = A_{v}[[R_{i}|R_{L}]]

Av is the gain from the amplifier’s own input terminal. Avs is from the source and includes the input divider. The current-gain formula is valid when Av is referenced to the input terminal (vo/vi); if it is referenced to the source, first include Ri/(Rs + Ri).

Why Ai = AvRi/RL: io = vo/RL and ii = vi/Ri, so the ratio is (vo/vi)(Ri/RL).

Ri is mainly the first stage’s input resistance. Ro is mainly the last stage’s output resistance (RC ∥ ro for a CE stage; about 1/gm-ish for a follower).

Worked Problem 4 (notes): two-stage CE with loading

gm1 = 40 mS, RC1 = 4.7 kΩ, ro1 = 80 kΩ, Ri2 = 3 kΩ. gm2 = 35 mS, RC2 = 3.3 kΩ, ro2 = 60 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ. Ri = 4 kΩ, Rs = 1 kΩ.

RC1′ = 4.7k ∥ 80k ∥ 3k = 1.790 kΩ → Av1 = −(0.04)(1790) = −71.6.

RC2′ = 3.3k ∥ 60k ∥ 10k = 2.383 kΩ → Av2 = −(0.035)(2383) = −83.4.

Av = (−71.6)(−83.4) = +5.97 × 10³. Avs = 5972 × 4/(1 + 4) = 4.78 × 10³. Ro ≈ 3.3k ∥ 60k = 3.13 kΩ. Ai = 5972 × 4k/10k = 2.39 × 10³.

Without loading, stage 1 would have claimed −(0.04)(4.7k ∥ 80k) = −178 instead of −71.6. That is the cost of ignoring Ri2.

4.2 Bandwidth of a cascade

Each first-order stage contributes its own low-frequency and high-frequency pole. For n identical stages, the overall response at frequency f (normalised to mid-band) is the single-stage response raised to the power n. Set it equal to 1/√2 and solve.

Derivation (the notes give the result)

One stage, upper pole: |A|/Amid = [1 + (f/fH,s)²]−1/2. For n stages: [1 + (f/fH,s)²]−n/2. At the overall −3 dB point this equals 2−1/2:

[1 + (f_{H}/f_{H,s})²]^{n} = 2  ⟹  (f_{H}/f_{H,s})² = 2^{1/n} − 1

and by the same steps for the lower pole (fL,s/fL)² = 21/n − 1.

f_{H} = f_{H,s}√(2^{1/n} − 1),    f_{L} = [[f_{L,s}|√(2^{1/n} − 1)]]

The shrinkage factor √(21/n − 1) is 1 for n = 1, 0.644 for n = 2, 0.510 for n = 3, 0.435 for n = 4. So two stages keep only 64% of the single-stage fH, and fL rises by 1/0.644 = 1.55 times.

Worked Problem 5 (notes): two identical stages

fL,s = 50 Hz, fH,s = 100 kHz.

fL = 50/√(21/2 − 1) = 77.7 Hz. fH = 100 kHz × √(21/2 − 1) = 64.4 kHz. BW ≈ 64.4 kHz − 0.078 kHz = 64.3 kHz.

The exact formulas are for identical first-order stages. For quick qualitative reasoning with unequal stages, the stage with the highest fL and the stage with the lowest fH dominate (the “dominant-stage idea”).

  1. Block diagram with Rs, stage 1, stage 2, RL (2).
  2. Loading: RC1′ = RC1 ∥ ro1 ∥ Ri2 (2).
  3. Av, Avs, Ai with formulas (3).
  4. Ri from first stage, Ro from last stage (1).

Quick check 1: three identical stages, each Av = 20 dB, fH,s = 200 kHz. Gain and fH?

60 dB overall. fH = 200 kHz × √(21/3 − 1) = 200 × 0.510 = 102 kHz.

Quick check 2: why not multiply the unloaded gains?

Because stage 1 drives Ri2 and its gain is then −gm1(RC1 ∥ ro1 ∥ Ri2), which is smaller. Using unloaded gains over-estimates the total.

Topic 5

Choice of transistor configuration in a cascade

Notes: Section 5 · 4–5 mark question
Intuition

Each configuration has a personality. CE gives gain. CC gives a buffer (voltage gain just under 1, high Ri, low Ro). CB has a low Ri and a good high-frequency response. Pick each stage for the job it has to do. The stages in a cascade need not all be the same.

ConfigurationVoltage gainRiRoWhy chosen
CEHigh, invertingModerateModerate to highMain voltage-gain stage
CC (emitter follower)Near unity, no inversionHighLowInput/output buffer, load driver
CBHigh, no inversionLowHighHigh-frequency stage; upper transistor of a cascode
DarlingtonNear unity as followerVery highLowVery high current gain and high Ri
Cascode (CE–CB)HighModerateHighHigh gain with improved high-frequency response

Typical cascades

  • CE → CE: high overall voltage gain; the standard RC-coupled voltage amplifier.
  • CE → CC: voltage gain, then a low-Ro buffer so that a low-resistance load does not collapse the gain.
  • CC → CE: a high-Ri front end for a weak, high-resistance source, then voltage gain.
  • CE → CB: the cascode (Topic 10) for high gain and wide bandwidth.
  • Darlington → CE or Darlington output stage: when the source cannot supply much input current.
Exam use

When a question gives a source resistance and a load resistance and asks “which configuration for each stage”, answer with the table: high-Rs source → CC/Darlington first; small RL → CC/power stage last; gain in the middle with CE.

Quick check: a 100 kΩ sensor drives an 8 Ω speaker. Sketch the chain.

CC/Darlington (high Ri, not loading the sensor) → CE (voltage gain) → CC or power stage (low Ro to drive 8 Ω). In practice the speaker stage is transformer-coupled or an emitter-follower power stage.

Topic 6

RC-coupled multistage amplifier

Asked: Summer 2019 Q1 (a) “Derive mid-band voltage gain of RC-coupled amplifier” (6) and Q1 (b) numerical (4)Notes: Section 6; Worked Problems 6 and 7

6.1 The circuit and what each part does

  • Stage 1 amplifies the input voltage. C3 carries the AC to stage 2 but blocks stage 1’s DC collector voltage, so each stage is biased independently.
  • Stage 2 gives more gain. C5 passes the amplified AC to the load and blocks DC.
  • C2, C4 bypass the emitter resistors so the AC gain is not reduced by emitter degeneration.

6.2 Derivation of the mid-band gain

At mid-band every external capacitor is a short and every junction capacitor is open. So the emitters of both transistors are at AC ground, and Figure 9 reduces to two hybrid-π CE stages with the divider resistors in parallel.

  1. Input resistances. Ri1 = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ rπ1 and Ri2 = R5 ∥ R6 ∥ rπ2.
  2. Loaded collector resistances. RC1′ = R3 ∥ ro1 ∥ Ri2 (stage 1 is loaded by stage 2) and RC2′ = R7 ∥ ro2 ∥ RL.
  3. Stage gains. Each transistor gives ic = gmvπ, and the current flows in the collector load. So Av1 = −gm1RC1′ and Av2 = −gm2RC2′.
  4. Overall. vo/vb1 = Av1Av2. From the source: vo/vs = [Ri1/(rs + Ri1)]Av1Av2.
  5. Terminal resistances (first order). Ri = Ri1 and Ro ≈ R7 ∥ ro2.
A_{v} = (−g_{m1}R_{C1}′)(−g_{m2}R_{C2}′),   [[v_{o}|v_{s}]] = [[R_{i1}|r_{s}+R_{i1}]]A_{v1}A_{v2}
Worked Problem 6 (notes): mid-band analysis

Both transistors: IC = 1 mA, β = 100, gm = 40 mS, rπ = 2.5 kΩ, ro = 100 kΩ. R1 = R5 = 47 kΩ, R2 = R6 = 10 kΩ, R3 = R7 = 4.7 kΩ, rs = 600 Ω, RL = 10 kΩ.

Ri1 = Ri2 = 47k ∥ 10k ∥ 2.5k = 1.918 kΩ.

RC1′ = 4.7k ∥ 100k ∥ 1.918k = 1.344 kΩ → Av1 = −53.8. RC2′ = 4.7k ∥ 100k ∥ 10k = 3.098 kΩ → Av2 = −123.9.

Av = +6.66 × 10³. vo/vs = 6662 × 1.918/(0.6 + 1.918) = 5.08 × 10³. Ro ≈ 4.7k ∥ 100k = 4.49 kΩ.

The notes warn that this is a small-signal result: signal swing, nonlinearity and bias headroom limit the usable gain in practice.

6.3 Choosing all five capacitors

The notes extend the single-stage rule to each capacitor of Figure 9:

C_{1} ≥ [[1|2πf_{p1}(r_{s}+R_{i1})]],   C_{3} ≥ [[1|2πf_{p3}(R_{o1}+R_{i2})]],   C_{5} ≥ [[1|2πf_{p5}(R_{o2}+R_{L})]]
C_{2} ≥ [[1|2πf_{p2}R_{E1,seen}]],   R_{E1,seen} = R_{4} ∥ [[r_{π1}+R_{B1}|β_{1}+1]],   R_{B1} = r_{s} ∥ R_{1} ∥ R_{2}
C_{4} ≥ [[1|2πf_{p4}R_{E2,seen}]],   R_{E2,seen} = R_{8} ∥ [[r_{π2}+R_{B2}|β_{2}+1]],   R_{B2} = R_{5} ∥ R_{6} ∥ R_{o1}

Here Ro1 ≈ R3 ∥ ro1 and Ro2 ≈ R7 ∥ ro2. The base resistance of stage 2 seen from its emitter is the previous stage’s output resistance in parallel with the divider. For a specified overall fL, assign each individual pole a little below it.

Worked Problem 7 (notes): all five capacitors for 10 Hz poles

Same amplifier as Problem 6, R4 = R8 = 1 kΩ.

C1 = 1/[2π(10)(0.6k + 1.918k)] = 6.32 µF. C3 = 1/[2π(10)(4.489k + 1.918k)] = 2.48 µF. C5 = 1/[2π(10)(4.489k + 10k)] = 1.10 µF.

RE1,seen = 29.4 Ω → C2 = 541 µF. RB2 = 47k ∥ 10k ∥ 4.489k = 2.907 kΩ, RE2,seen = 1k ∥ (2.5k + 2.907k)/101 = 50.8 Ω → C4 = 313 µF.

Standard choices: C1 = 10 µF, C3 = 3.3 µF, C5 = 1.5 µF, C2 = 560 µF, C4 = 330 µF. A larger standard value moves the pole lower.

6.4 Design procedure

  1. Decide the total gain and split it between the stages.
  2. Choose IC of each stage and put VCE near mid-swing.
  3. Choose RC and RE to establish the Q-points.
  4. Choose the base divider of each stage.
  5. Check interstage loading and revise the gain.
  6. Choose coupling and bypass capacitors for the required fL.
  7. Check the transistor’s high-frequency capability (fT, Miller effect) if a wide fH is needed.

A route that fits the notes: Rb = R1 ∥ R2 = 9.17 kΩ; rb′e = hfe/gm = 50/0.04 = 1.25 kΩ; Ri = Rb ∥ hie = 9.17k ∥ 1.2k = 1.06 kΩ; Ro = RC ∥ 1/hoe (the hoe exponent and RC value are unreadable in my scan, so substitute them from your paper).

Quick check 1: why is each stage biased independently?

C3 blocks DC, so stage 1’s collector voltage does not change stage 2’s base bias. Each stage has its own divider and RE.

Quick check 2: two CE stages: phase between vo and vb1?

Each inverts 180°, so the product is positive: in phase.

Topic 7

Direct-coupled amplifier

Asked: Summer 2019 Q1 OR (a) “How is the direct-coupled amplifier developed?” (6)Notes: Section 7; Worked Problem 8

7.1 How it develops from the RC-coupled amplifier

Intuition (use this as the “development” story)
  1. Start with the two-stage RC-coupled amplifier. The interstage capacitor C3 blocks DC, so the lower cut-off is non-zero, and DC or very slow signals cannot pass.
  2. Remove C3 and the stage-2 divider (R5, R6 in Figure 9) and connect collector of Q1 directly to base of Q2. Now the collector DC level of Q1 is the base bias of Q2.
  3. Q2’s base current is drawn through Q1’s collector resistor, so it must be included in the R3 current.
  4. Result: the interstage path passes both AC and DC. The price is that bias and temperature drift now propagate from stage to stage.

7.2 DC design equations

V_{B1} ≈ V_{CC}[[R_{2}|R_{1}+R_{2}]],   V_{E1} = V_{B1} − V_{BE1},   I_{E1} ≈ [[V_{E1}|R_{4}]],   I_{C1} ≈ α_{1}I_{E1}
V_{B2} = V_{C1} = V_{CC} − R_{3}(I_{C1} + I_{B2}),   V_{E2} = V_{B2} − V_{BE2},   I_{E2} ≈ [[V_{E2}|R_{6}]],   I_{B2} ≈ [[I_{E2}|β_{2}+1]]
V_{C2} = V_{CC} − I_{C2}R_{5}

Because IB2 depends on VB2, which depends on IB2, solve it once as one linear equation. A first pass may neglect IB2 (VC1 ≈ VCC − IC1R3), then correct it.

7.3 Mid-band small-signal analysis

With C2, C3 bypassing R4, R6, and no divider on Q2, rπ2 alone loads the collector of Q1:

R_{C1}′ = R_{3} ∥ r_{o1} ∥ r_{π2},   R_{C2}′ = R_{5} ∥ r_{o2} ∥ R_{L},   A_{v} ≈ (−g_{m1}R_{C1}′)(−g_{m2}R_{C2}′)
Worked Problem 8 (notes): DC analysis of Figure 10

VCC = 12 V, R1 = 82 kΩ, R2 = 18 kΩ, R3 = 3.3 kΩ, R4 = 1 kΩ, R5 = 680 Ω, R6 = 1.5 kΩ, β1 = β2 = 100.

VB1 ≈ 12 × 18/100 = 2.16 V → VE1 = 1.46 V → IC1 ≈ (100/101)(1.46 mA) = 1.446 mA.

VB2 = 12 − 3.3k[IC1 + (VB2 − 0.7)/(1.5k × 101)] ≈ 7.09 V, VE2 ≈ 6.39 V → IE2 ≈ 4.26 mA, IC2 ≈ 4.22 mA.

VC2 = 12 − (4.22 mA)(680) = 9.13 V, VCE2 ≈ 2.74 V. Both transistors keep VCE headroom (VCE1 = VB2 − VE1 ≈ 5.6 V).

Drift and the fL statement (the notes are careful here)
  • Drift: a small change in VBE or IC in stage 1 is amplified by stage 2 and looks like a signal. Treat the circuit as one DC system.
  • Lower cut-off: the direct connection adds no interstage pole. But the circuit in Figure 10 still has C1, C2, C3, C4, so fL is not zero. fL = 0 only for a fully DC-coupled amplifier with the external coupling capacitors also removed.

Topic 8

Transformer-coupled amplifier

Asked: Summer 2019 Q3 (b) reflected load for 150 Ω and a 5 : 2 transformerNotes: Section 8; Worked Problem 9

8.1 Why a transformer?

Intuition

A transistor delivers its best power into a load of some kΩ (a few hundred ohms to a few kΩ for a typical power stage). A loudspeaker is 4–16 Ω. Connect them directly and almost no power transfers. A transformer is a “gearbox” for impedance: it reflects the 8 Ω speaker to look like a few kΩ at the collector. It also gives DC isolation between the collector circuit and the load.

8.2 Reflected load and turns ratio

For an ideal transformer with n = N1/N2: voltages scale as V1/V2 = n, currents inversely, I2/I1 = n.

Derivation

The primary sees R′ = V1/I1. Substitute V1 = nV2 and I1 = I2/n:

R_{L}′ = [[V_{1}|I_{1}]] = [[nV_{2}|I_{2}/n]] = n²[[V_{2}|I_{2}]] = n²R_{L}
R_{L}′ = n²R_{L} = (N_{1}/N_{2})²R_{L},    n = √[[R_{L}′|R_{L}]]

With the emitter at AC ground and an ideal transformer in mid-band, the collector load is RC′ ≈ RL′ ∥ ro, so

A_{v,p} ≈ −g_{m}R_{C}′ (at the primary),    A_{v,sec} ≈ [[A_{v,p}|n]] (at the secondary)

The secondary voltage is smaller than the primary by n when n > 1 (a step-down). The power transfer, not the voltage, is the goal.

8.3 Frequency limits of the transformer

  • Low frequency: the magnetizing inductance Lm sits in parallel with the reflected load and shunts the signal. First-order corner: fL ≈ RL′/(2πLm).
  • High frequency: leakage inductance and winding capacitance limit the response.
Worked Problem 9 (notes)

An 8 Ω load is to appear as 2 kΩ at the collector. Required fL = 50 Hz, gm = 40 mS, ro = 100 kΩ.

n = √(2000/8) = 15.8 ≈ 16 : 1. Lm = 2000/(2π × 50) = 6.37 H. RC′ = 2k ∥ 100k = 1.961 kΩ, Av,p = −(0.04)(1961) = −78.4, Av,sec = −78.4/15.8 = −4.96.

A practical transformer would have a turns ratio near 16 : 1 and at least about 6.4 H of magnetizing inductance.

Summer 2019 question

RL = 150 Ω, turns ratio 5 : 2, so n = 2.5 and RL′ = (2.5)²(150) = 6.25 × 150 = 937.5 Ω (reflected to the primary).

Transformer coupling is most attractive when power transfer and impedance matching matter more than size, cost or very wide bandwidth.

Topic 9

Darlington amplifier

Notes: Section 9; Worked Problem 10
Intuition

Take two transistors and let the emitter of the first drive the base of the second. The tiny base current of Q2 is multiplied by β2 in its emitter, and that whole emitter current becomes the base current of Q1, which multiplies it again by β1. Total current gain is roughly β1β2. With β = 100 each, one base electron controls 10 000 collector electrons.

9.1 Compound current gain

I_{B1} = I_{E2} = (β_{2}+1)I_{B2},    I_{E1} = (β_{1}+1)I_{B1} = (β_{1}+1)(β_{2}+1)I_{B2}
β_{D} ≡ [[I_{C1}+I_{C2}|I_{B2}]] = β_{1}β_{2} + β_{1} + β_{2} ≈ β_{1}β_{2}

The product approximation is adequate when both β are large. The base–emitter voltage of the pair is about two diode drops (1.2–1.4 V for silicon), so the output emitter sits two VBE below the input base:

V_{E1} ≈ V_{B2} − V_{BE2} − V_{BE1},    I_{E1,Q} ≈ [[V_{E1}|R_{3}]]

9.2 Input resistance and gain

At mid-band C2 is a short, so the emitter of Q1 sees RE′ = R3 ∥ RL. Work backwards from the output along the signal path:

  1. Into the base of Q1: Rin,B1 = rπ1 + (β1 + 1)RE′. This is the load on the emitter of Q2.
  2. Into the base of Q2: Rin,B2 = rπ2 + (β2 + 1)Rin,B1. A follower multiplies whatever is at its emitter by (β + 1).
  3. With the bias divider in parallel: Ri = (R1 ∥ R2) ∥ Rin,B2.
A_{v1} ≈ [[(β_{1}+1)R_{E}′|r_{π1}+(β_{1}+1)R_{E}′]],   A_{v2} ≈ [[(β_{2}+1)R_{in,B1}|r_{π2}+(β_{2}+1)R_{in,B1}]],   A_{v} = A_{v1}A_{v2} ≈ 1⁻
Worked Problem 10 (notes)

VCC = 12 V, R1 = 100 kΩ, R2 = 33 kΩ, R3 = 470 Ω, RL = 1 kΩ, β2 = 80 (input transistor), β1 = 100 (output transistor). rπ1 ≈ 0.752 kΩ, rπ2 ≈ 60.9 kΩ.

VB2 ≈ 12 × 33/133 = 2.98 V → VE1 ≈ 2.98 − 1.40 = 1.58 V → IE1 ≈ 1.58/470 = 3.36 mA.

RE′ = 470 ∥ 1k = 319.7 Ω. Rin,B1 = 0.752k + 101(319.7) = 33.0 kΩ. Rin,B2 = 60.9k + 81(33.0k) = 2.74 MΩ.

Ri = (100k ∥ 33k) ∥ 2.74M = 24.6 kΩ. Av ≈ (0.977)(0.978) = 0.955.

The intrinsic input resistance is megohms, but the bias divider caps the actual Ri at 24.6 kΩ. That is the classic Darlington design lesson: bias resistors waste the high Ri, so bootstrap or use a large R1∥R2 (standard remark, not in the notes).

  • Very high current gain and high input resistance; low output resistance; voltage gain just below 1.
  • Needs about 2VBE of drive; slower than a single transistor (from the comparison table in the notes).
  • Check the power in Q1: it carries almost all the load current.

  4. Features and uses; VBE drop of ≈ 1.2–1.4 V (2).

50 × 50 + 50 + 50 = 2600 (the approximation gives 2500, only 4% less).

Topic 10

Cascode amplifier

Asked: Summer 2019 Q1 (b) “What is a cascode amplifier?” (4)Notes: Section 10; Worked Problem 11
Intuition

The problem with a CE stage is Miller: its collector swings a lot, and that big swing multiplies Cμ at the input. The cure: let the collector of the input transistor not move. Put a second transistor in common-base on top. Its emitter is a very low impedance (1/gm), so Q1’s collector is almost a short to AC ground and barely swings. Q1 still turns input voltage into current (gm1vπ), Q2 passes that current to the collector load R4, and the big voltage swing happens up at Q2’s collector, away from the input.

Important AC condition (the notes stress this)

For Q2 to be a true common-base transistor its base must be at AC ground: bypass the Q2 base node with a large capacitor to ground (or hold it with a low-impedance bias source). All the gain, output-resistance and Miller expressions below assume this. If the Q2 base moves with the signal, the simple cascode equations no longer apply.

10.1 DC bias

The series divider R1–R2–R3 sets both base voltages (neglecting base current for a first pass):

V_{B1} ≈ V_{CC}[[R_{3}|R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}]],   V_{B2} ≈ V_{CC}[[R_{2}+R_{3}|R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}]],   I ≈ I_{C1} ≈ I_{C2} ≈ [[V_{B1}−V_{BE1}|R_{5}]]
V_{C1} = V_{E2} ≈ V_{B2} − V_{BE2},   V_{CE1} ≈ V_{C1} − V_{E1},   V_{C2} = V_{CC} − IR_{4},   V_{CE2} = V_{C2} − V_{E2}

Both VCE values must stay in the forward-active region over the whole swing. Two stacked transistors need more supply headroom than one CE stage.

10.2 Gain, output resistance and Miller effect

A_{v} ≈ −g_{m1}R_{C}′,   R_{C}′ = R_{4} ∥ R_{L} ∥ R_{out,casc}
R_{out,casc} ≈ r_{o1} + r_{o2} + g_{m2}r_{o1}r_{o2} ≈ g_{m}r_{o}²   (matched devices)
Why Rout is so large (added reasoning)

Look into the collector of Q2. A change vx there makes a small change in Q2’s emitter voltage. Because Q1’s collector current is held constant by its fixed base voltage, that emitter voltage change is only ixro1. Q2’s own gm2 then amplifies the effect of ro1, so ro1 appears multiplied by (1 + gm2ro2) in series with ro2: Rout ≈ ro2 + ro1(1 + gm2ro2), the notes’ result. The practical effect: the transistor behaves almost like an ideal current source, so RC′ is set by R4 and RL.

A_{vb1} ≡ [[v_{c1}|v_{π1}]] ≈ −[[g_{m1}|g_{m2}]] ≈ −1,    C_{in} ≈ C_{π1} + 2C_{μ1} + C_{stray}

The gain from Q1’s base-emitter voltage to Q1’s collector is only about −1. So Miller multiplies Cμ1 by 1 − (−1) = 2, not by 1 + |Av| ≈ 100.

Worked Problem 11 (notes): bias, gain and Miller reduction

VCC = 12 V, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = R3 = 22 kΩ, R4 = 3.3 kΩ, R5 = 2.2 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ. VBE = 0.7 V, gm1 = gm2 = 40 mS, ro = 100 kΩ, Cπ1 = 25 pF, Cμ1 = 4 pF, Cstray = 3 pF.

VB1 = 12 × 22/91 = 2.90 V, VB2 = 12 × 44/91 = 5.80 V, VE1 = 2.20 V → I = 2.20/2.2k = 1.00 mA.

VC1 = VE2 = 5.80 − 0.70 = 5.10 V → VCE1 = 2.90 V. VC2 = 12 − 3.3 = 8.70 V → VCE2 = 3.60 V.

Rout,casc ≈ 100k + 100k + (0.04)(100k)² ≈ 400 MΩ. RC′ = 3.3k ∥ 10k ∥ 400M = 2.48 kΩ → Av ≈ −(0.04)(2480) = −99.2.

Cin,casc ≈ 25 + 2(4) + 3 = 36 pF. For a plain CE stage with the same gain, Cin,CE ≈ 25 + 4(1 + 99.2) + 3 ≈ 429 pF. Same gain, about 12 times less input capacitance, so fH improves by roughly the same factor.

10.3 Design steps and when to choose a cascode

  1. Choose IC from the gain, noise and power considerations; bias Q1 for it.
  2. Choose VB2 so that Q1’s collector sits at a nearly constant voltage with enough VCE1.
  3. Choose R4 for the gain and swing.
  4. Check both transistors stay in forward-active over the swing, and check the supply headroom.

CE or cascode? Choose a plain CE stage when simplicity and gain are the aims. Choose a cascode when the same gain is needed with a better high-frequency response and less input–output feedback.

  • High voltage gain like a good CE stage; very high output resistance; reduced Miller effect; better isolation between input and output; more supply voltage and more components needed.

  2. 10 marks, add: circuit with bias ladder and AC-grounded Q2 base (2), DC equations (2), Av ≈ −gm1RC′ and Rout ≈ gmro² (3), Miller reduction with numbers (2), merits and demerits (1).
Quick check 1: gain of the Q1 stage alone in a matched cascode?

About −gm1/gm2 = −1, because Q2’s emitter resistance 1/gm2 is the load.

Quick check 2: why the extra supply voltage?

Two transistors are stacked in series, so VCC must cover two VCE values, the emitter resistor drop and the collector resistor drop.

Comparison and design workflow

Seven-step design workflow

  1. Start from the required gain, input resistance, output resistance, bandwidth and signal swing.
  2. Select stage configurations by the function of each stage.
  3. Complete the DC bias design first.
  4. Calculate the loaded mid-band gains and verify the total gain.
  5. Choose coupling and bypass components and check the low-frequency response.
  6. Check high-frequency limits, especially Miller effect in high-gain CE stages.
  7. Check signal swing, power dissipation, tolerances and temperature stability.
Final design perspective (notes)

A good multistage amplifier is not simply a collection of high-gain stages. Source loading, interstage loading, output drive, DC bias, frequency response and signal swing must all be satisfactory at the same time.
